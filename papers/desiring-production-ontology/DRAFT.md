# DMML as a Desiring-Production Ontology

*Philosophy of technology / STS framing. Exact pincites for the Deleuze/Guattari and Simondon material are marked pending below and are needed before submission; the drafting history behind this text lives in this repository's own commit log and `dev-journal/`, not in the paper.*

## Abstract

DMML is a small declarative language for text-adventure world models: a `commit` block asserts what a world's state consumed and what it produced, and nothing else is a primitive. This paper argues DMML's commit model is a real, checkable structural correspondence to Deleuze and Guattari's desiring-production, not a decorative metaphor — and is equally careful about where that correspondence runs out. Its connectivity is rhizomatic rather than arborescent in Deleuze and Guattari's own specified sense, a claim that survives comparison not only to git and blockchain but to the open web and ordinary linked data, via a further, narrowly-stated property: DMML's edges cannot dangle unnoticed, which the open web's can, though this is grounding rather than a guarantee of semantic fidelity. We then argue DMML's own grammar is better read through Deleuze and Guattari's apparatus of strata, double articulation, and deterritorialization/reterritorialization on and off a plane of consistency than through Simondon's individuation, which names something real but converges on a stable individual DMML's own multiplicity keeps deferring; a small, runnable simulation (a "pantheon" of independently-authored commits) is used throughout as concrete evidence rather than asserted in the abstract. On this reading DMML is not merely rhizomatic but auto-recombinant: its own accumulated multiplicity is a first-class input to further production, an ordinary act of the grammar rather than a special case. Charge, on this reading, is not a property of a reconciled or unreconciled state; it inheres in the fact of change itself, what Deleuze and Guattari call speed. We indicate the direction this points rather than claim to have proven it arrives.

## 1. The mechanism, before any philosophy

A DMML world is a directed history of `commit` blocks. Each commit declares what it `consumes` — references to prior facts or nodes it depends on — and what it `produces` — new triples asserted into the world's graph. There is no primitive for editing a fact in place: an attribute change is itself a commit that consumes the old value and produces a new one, and the old commit's data stays physically in the history, only superseded for the purpose of reading current state. Retraction is the same shape with an empty `produces`.

Two distinct things are true here and should not be run together. First, at the level of the portable reference implementation itself: a `consumes` entry that cites a fact nothing actually produced is a no-op, not an error — the interpreter's own retraction step simply finds nothing to retract and moves on, a deliberate, formally-specified design decision (`fact_retraction_fails_open`), not an oversight. A commit that cites something fabricated is still accepted; it just fails to retract what it claimed to. Second, and separately: whether a commit gets *admitted into a world's history at all* is a substrate-specific question this portable layer does not answer on its own — written-world's atproto-backed deployment gates admission on a real, live compare-and-swap against its repository (issue #53), which is where anything resembling "was this citation genuine at write time" actually gets enforced, if it does. What both layers agree on, and what genuinely holds regardless of which is in view: neither one verifies that a reference is *current* (the most recent state of whatever it points to), nor that a new commit's `produces` is *semantically consistent* with what it consumes beyond naming something real. An older reference is accepted, not rejected as stale. Two commits can cite the same real prior fact and produce contradictory claims about what follows from it, and nothing at either layer stops either one. What can be said honestly, and no more strongly: citation in DMML is checkable, not policed — by a reader who goes and looks, manually or with tooling this paper does not claim exists, not automatically by the grammar itself. A fabricated or stale reference is not silently laundered into truth for that reader, but making sure it never enters the log in the first place is substrate work, not a property of the grammar.

A **petition** is a structured request raised against something underdetermined — a player asks what is behind a locked door, and no commit yet answers it — which an outside party (in practice, an AI acting as an author, operating under a persistent, signing identity rather than as a stateless one-off process) resolves. The resolution is itself a commit: it consumes the petition and produces the new fact, permanently linked back to the request. Nothing in DMML's grammar or interpreter computes an expected answer from the request; the request only establishes that some production is now outstanding, not what its content will be.

Concretely, one lifecycle: (1) a player commit `produces` a `Petition` node asking what is behind `door/1`; (2) an author commit `consumes` that petition and `produces` a new room, `room/9`, plus a fact linking `door/1` to it; (3) a later, unrelated commit — the player unlocking `door/1` — `consumes` the door's prior state and cites the room-linking fact from (2) as part of what it builds on. Step (3) is checked only for whether the fact it cites was genuinely produced in (2), not for whether (2)'s content was itself the "right" answer to (1)'s question, and not against whether some other commit has since produced a conflicting claim about `door/1`. That last gap is real and is what Section 4 turns on.

## 2. Desiring-machines, precisely

Deleuze and Guattari open *Anti-Oedipus* (1972; trans. Hurley, Seem, and Lane, 1977) with a claim introduced through examples before it is introduced through definition: "Everywhere it is machines — real ones, not figurative ones." The breast is a machine that produces milk; the mouth is a machine coupled to it, and immediately multifunctional — eating, speaking, breathing, a machine connecting into more than one production at once.<sup>[pincite pending, standard Minnesota Press edition, ch. 1 §1, "The Desiring-Machines"]</sup> The chapter's organizing thesis is that desire is not correctly modeled as lack seeking satisfaction: desire *produces*, and what it produces is real, not fantasy compensating for an absence.<sup>[same]</sup>

The phrase "real ones, not figurative ones" is doing specific rhetorical work in the source that should not be borrowed uncritically: it is aimed at desire's own self-understanding, insisting desire is not merely staged or theatrical, not a claim that any literal piece of software instantiates the thesis. What can be claimed instead, more modestly: DMML's primitives can be checked against the production-not-lack thesis as a structural constraint, and the check either passes or it doesn't. Section 3 does that check.

## 3. What the check actually shows, and what it doesn't

A system built on lack-and-satisfaction would need some representation of a deficit that a later event fills — a slot whose shape is fixed in advance, with the later event's job being to match it. DMML's grammar has no such primitive at the level of the language itself: nothing in the interpreter computes an expected or required `produces` from a `consumes`. That is a real, checkable fact about the grammar.

It is also narrower than "DMML is free of lack." Lack does not require a `LACK` predicate to be operative — it can live entirely in *content*: a locked door, a quest structure, a goal state a player is working toward, are all lack-shaped regardless of what the underlying grammar's primitives are. A world authored in DMML could be saturated with deficit-and-completion structure at the content layer while the grammar underneath stays clean of any built-in lack primitive. The claim that actually survives: **DMML's grammar does not impose lack-and-satisfaction as its own operative logic; it does not prevent, and has no opinion about, authors choosing to write lack-shaped content on top of it.** A claim about what the ontology requires, not about what every possible world built on it will do.

A second complication: the petition's resolution is, in this project's current implementation, produced by a large language model sampling from a learned distribution over its training data — reasonably describable as selection from an extremely large menu of already-determined possibilities, close to the very thing this section distinguishes DMML from. Whether sampling from a learned distribution counts as production in Deleuze and Guattari's sense is a separate, genuinely open question about generative models generally, not something DMML's own design settles. The claim this section actually makes is scoped narrower and stays real regardless: whatever the resolver's own internal process is, DMML's *grammar and interpreter* do not themselves compute a canonical target the resolver is checked against. The system-level absence of a built-in canonical-answer function is the claim; what happens inside whatever black box supplies the answer is out of scope.

One further concession: the claim above should be read as checkable, not falsifiable in a stronger sense — the only actual falsifying event would be changing the software itself, a design commitment stated in conditional form, not an empirical hypothesis exposed to independent test. What can be said is narrower and more useful: this is a claim about what the current grammar requires, offered so a reader can check it against the actual interpreter code rather than take it on the paper's word.

One piece of design history is the strongest evidence here and deserves to be stated plainly: an earlier version of this project's own AI-driven content generation computed petition-like answers from a fixed content model, and produced content that felt derivative — flatly, "content that just re-asserts something already true." The fix, arrived at from game-design concerns before any philosophical framing was applied to it, was removing exactly the canonical-answer computation this section argues against. That the diagnosis converges with Deleuze and Guattari's own critique of representational, selection-based models of desire is a small, independently-arrived-at instance of a critique they share with a broader anti-representational tradition — discovered by engineering failure, not derived from the theory, and useful evidence for exactly that reason.

## 4. Multiplicity, strata, and the auto-recombinant rhizome

Simondon's transindividual names something real: the relation itself as the site at which participants are individuated, not a channel between two already-formed individuals.<sup>[pincite pending; framing checked against the Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews treatment of Muriel Combes' *Gilbert Simondon and the Philosophy of the Transindividual*, MIT Press]</sup> It still converges on a determinate outcome, though — a real, locatable individual, even one produced *through* relation rather than prior to it — and that is not well described by what a DMML petition's resolution actually does. Deleuze and Guattari have their own apparatus for exactly this — strata, double articulation, deterritorialization coupled with reterritorialization, playing out on and off what they call the plane of consistency — developed independently of Simondon and not needing him as scaffolding. That apparatus, not a checklist run against either thinker's criteria, is this section's actual register. A concrete artifact grounds the claims below throughout: `dmml/examples/pantheon.rs`, a small, runnable simulation built specifically to check them against real interpreter output rather than assert them in prose (per this project's own "try it first" discipline) — every structural claim in what follows names which of that file's checks it rests on.

### The connectivity is rhizomatic, not arborescent

Deleuze and Guattari's "Introduction: Rhizome" (*A Thousand Plateaus*, 1980; trans. Massumi, 1987) opens the book, not incidentally, with multiplicity as a starting condition rather than a derived complication: "The two of us wrote *Anti-Oedipus* together. Since each of us was several, there was already quite a crowd."<sup>[content verified against the opening of "Introduction: Rhizome" via convergent secondary sourcing; print pincite pending]</sup> The chapter names, among six real principles, three directly relevant here: **connection and heterogeneity** — any point of a rhizome can and must connect to any other, unlike a tree's links, fixed by descent from one root; **multiplicity** — a rhizome has no unity to add to or subtract from, which a tree's branching, however complex, still lacks, since every branch traces back to one trunk; and **cartography over tracing** — a tree *traces*, reproducing a pre-given genealogical order; a rhizome *maps*, constructing rather than reproducing.

Checked against this, git and blockchain are not the right comparisons for DMML, and not equivalent to each other either. A blockchain is fairly arborescent without much qualification: Nakamoto consensus exists to collapse competing forks to one canonical chain, a hard principle of unity a rhizome's multiplicity refuses. Git is a closer call: its real commit history is a directed acyclic graph, not strictly a tree — merge commits have multiple parents, and nothing forces convergence, so forks can simply persist. What's tree-like about git is convention (the single "main" branch), not the underlying structure. DMML's own connectivity reduces to neither, for a plainer reason than either comparison needs: **nothing in the grammar or reference interpreter enforces convergence on a `(subject, predicate)` pair at all.** There is no declared "one winner" policy to opt into or out of — no mechanism exists yet that would even let an author ask for one. Two independently-authored commits can assert incompatible facts about the same subject with zero coordination, and both remain real, permanent, individually citable history. This is demonstrated directly, not just asserted: `dmml/examples/pantheon.rs` builds exactly this case — three DID-authored commits (Helios, Selene, Eos) asserting different origins for the same node, none referencing any other — and the reference materializer accepts all three into the log without complaint. A `consumes` entry can also cite multiple prior facts at once, and `FactRef` addressing lets a commit cite one specific triple within any prior commit rather than only "the tip"; none of this is bounded to one repository the way even git's DAG is, since a foreign-DID quotation lets a commit in one sovereign world cite a node from an entirely separate repository as a first-person percept, a connection across a boundary git's object graph has no lightweight native mechanism for (a submodule is the closest git gets, and it pins a fixed SHA pointer into a foreign repository's history — a reference, not a copy — but still requires a separate fetch/checkout step to resolve; a DMML `FactRef` resolves the same way any other citation in the log does, no separate mechanism required).

Git and blockchain are not the hardest comparison, though. The hyperlinked web satisfies connection/heterogeneity, multiplicity, and cross-boundary linking about as well as DMML does; so does the Semantic Web's own RDF/linked-data model, which DMML's own subject-predicate-object graph structurally resembles; so does an academic citation network. If rhizomatic topology alone were what this argument needed, the open web would already be individuating at planetary scale, which is not a conclusion worth defending. The property that actually distinguishes DMML here is narrower than "connections can't lie," and needs to be stated at the strength it has and no further: the *commit* carrying a fabricated citation is accepted regardless, at the portable-crate level (Section 1) — but the citation itself does nothing, silently, rather than being treated as if it named something true, so a reader who actually checks a specific citation against the log (manually, or with tooling nothing in this paper claims already exists) finds the gap rather than a laundered fact. That is weaker than "connections can't lie" and is stated at that weaker strength deliberately. What is checkable, and what `pantheon.rs` also demonstrates directly: because nothing forces a single winner, a misreading or a rival claim does not get silently promoted to canonical status the way an unchecked hyperlink effectively can when nothing else contests it — it persists instead as one more citable, disputable production, findable and arguable rather than laundered into an accepted fact by default. `pantheon.rs`'s own council commit adds one further, genuinely distinct move: `sky/1`'s `canonicalOrigin` is declared on top of the still-fully-present, still-divergent `origin` history, as an ordinary, separate, revisable fact — not a rewrite of the underlying multiplicity, and not erasing Helios's, Selene's, or Eos's own commits, which remain independently citable by their own identifiers regardless of what canonicity says. The rhizome here is not one where every edge is beyond dispute. It is one where disputes themselves are structurally cheap to keep present rather than costly to preserve against a system actively trying to converge them.

### Strata, capture, and the plane of consistency

Deleuze and Guattari's own vocabulary for this is precise and technical, not a looser gloss on Simondon. A **stratum** is defined by **double articulation**: "Articulate twice, B-A, BA... The first articulation chooses or deducts, from unstable particle-flows, metastable... units *(substances)* upon which it imposes a statistical order of connections and successions *(forms)*," and "the second articulation establishes functional, compact, stable structures *(forms)*, and constructs the molar compounds in which these structures are simultaneously actualized *(substances)*."<sup>[verified against a direct read of "10,000 B.C.: The Geology of Morals," pp. 40–41, Massumi translation]</sup> Their own worked example is geological: sedimentation (the first articulation, statistically depositing material) and folding (the second, which "sets up a stable functional structure and effects the passage from sediment to sedimentary rock"). The content/expression vocabulary that names the two articulations is explicitly Hjelmslev's, adopted and generalized past its linguistic origin, not Deleuze and Guattari's own coinage.<sup>[same, p. 43]</sup> What strata capture flows *from* is what they call the **plane of consistency** or body without organs: "permeated by unformed, unstable matters, by flows in all directions... the body without organs is itself the plane of consistency, which becomes compact... at the level of the strata."<sup>[same, p. 40]</sup> On that plane, prior to capture, elements hold together without a pre-given hierarchy distinguishing them: "The plane of consistency knows nothing of differences in level... nothing of the distinction between contents and expressions... a semiotic fragment rubs shoulders with a chemical interaction... The plane of consistency is the abolition of all metaphor; all that consists is Real."<sup>[same, p. 69; the chapter's own contrastive pairing is plane of consistency/Planomenon vs. strata/Ecumenon, not "plane of organization" — that term belongs to a different plateau and should not be imported here]</sup> Crucially, capture is never a single, final event: deterritorialization (a flow breaking from a code) and reterritorialization (its recoding onto a new stratum) are a coupled pair, not deterritorialization alone — "deterritorialization... is always relative, and has reterritorialization as its flipside or complement."<sup>[same, p. 54]</sup>

`pantheon.rs` makes this concrete rather than leaving it an analogy asserted in prose. Helios's, Selene's, and Eos's independent commits — three DID-authored claims about `sky/1`'s origin, coordinated with each other not at all — sit on something structurally like the plane of consistency: heterogeneous, uncoded, with nothing in the grammar imposing an order of precedence between them (the rhizome subsection's own point, Check 1–2 above). Nyx's commit does something double articulation describes well: its `consumes` block performs a first-articulation-shaped move, selecting specific prior facts out of the wider, uncoordinated flow of everything any DID could have asserted about anything; its `produces` block performs a second, folding that selection into one new, stable triple (`duskweave`) that existed in none of the three inputs. It should come as no surprise, and is worth saying plainly rather than defending against, that an ordinary SQL `JOIN`, a git merge, or a SPARQL `CONSTRUCT` query does something with the same shape — reads several existing records, writes one new one back. Deleuze and Guattari are themselves explicit that double articulation is a *general* structural pattern, not a linguistic one — the content/expression vocabulary they use to name it is "general characteristics of strata," adopted from Hjelmslev and explicitly generalized past his own linguistic use of it, and their own worked example for it is geological (sedimentation, then folding), not linguistic at all.<sup>[same, p. 43]</sup> A structural pattern general enough to organize geology should turn up all over ordinary computing too, precisely because ordinary computing is full of processes that capture a selection from some wider set and fold it into a new stable structure — finding this pattern in SQL and git is confirmation the analogy is reading the primary text's own claim to generality correctly, not a coincidence threatening to undermine it. What is worth asking, then, is not whether DMML instantiates double articulation — plenty of things do — but what DMML's own instance of it is actually doing that a SQL `JOIN`'s or a git merge's is not: the facts Nyx folds together are, unlike an arbitrary SQL row, individually grounded against a real production history (Section 1) rather than merely present in a table; unlike a git merge's parents, they can originate from entirely separate sovereign repositories with no shared authority requiring anyone's permission first; and unlike a SPARQL `CONSTRUCT` over an open, unverified graph, nothing about Nyx's synthesis needed the underlying claims to have been reconciled, ranked, or deduplicated first. This paper calls that combination — ordinary double articulation running through grounded, sovereignty-crossing, non-convergent connectivity — **auto-recombinant**: a rhizome whose own accumulated multiplicity is a first-class input to further production, with no special primitive required beyond `consumes` and `produces` aimed recursively at the graph's own history (`pantheon.rs` Check 3). Nyx's synthesis is a real instance of the deterritorialization/reterritorialization pair on that same reading: Helios's, Selene's, and Eos's separate claims are deterritorialized — broken from standing alone as the sole word on `sky/1`'s origin — and reterritorialized onto a new stratum, `duskweave`, that none singly constituted. The council's later commit, declaring `canonicalOrigin` on top of `origin` without touching or erasing it (Check 4), is a second, further reterritorialization at one remove: a coded, practically-operative layer added to the plane, not a rewrite of what the plane still holds. Helios's, Selene's, and Eos's own commits remain, per Check 2, fully present and independently citable throughout — captured by neither Nyx's synthesis nor the council's declaration, only added to.

The same point holds, and is worth extending rather than treated as a separate threat to answer, for append-only, query-and-recombine data stores like Datomic, event-sourced systems generally, and RDF stores under SPARQL — all read arbitrary historical data and write new assertions back without discarding anything, the same append-and-recombine shape `pantheon.rs` exercises, and for the same reason just given: this is what capture-and-recode generally looks like when implemented in software, not a coincidence specific to this comparison. What such a system does not have, structurally, is the same triad named above: grounded citation checkable against a real production history rather than an internally-consistent-but-otherwise-unverified store; connectivity that crosses sovereign, independently-authored repositories with no shared administrative authority (an event-sourced system is, ordinarily, one system, one authority, whatever its internal event log looks like); and a default of non-convergence rather than an application layer that could, at any point, choose to enforce one. DMML's distinctiveness is that combination running through an ordinary, unprivileged grammar primitive — not the append-and-recombine shape by itself, which this paper is glad to concede widely, since conceding it is what the primary text's own claim to generality predicts.

One further distinction from the same primary text is worth stating honestly rather than reaching past it. Deleuze and Guattari separate *relative* deterritorialization, always eventually reterritorialized somewhere, from a rarer *absolute* deterritorialization tied to the plane of consistency's own "destratification," and are explicit that the difference is not one of speed or degree but of "nature" — whether a movement proceeds by articulated, segmentary steps or "jumps from one singularity to another following a nondecomposable, nonsegmentary line."<sup>[same, pp. 55–56]</sup> Nyx's synthesis and the council's declaration are both, on this reading, ordinary *relative* deterritorializations — each one real, each one checkable, each one still eventually recodable by a further commit (nothing stops a fourth deity's commit from consuming Nyx's and the council's productions together and folding them into something newer still). What is not shown here, and this paper does not claim to have shown, is anything answering to *absolute* deterritorialization in Deleuze and Guattari's stronger sense. The full commit log — the totality of what any DID could ever cite or recombine, never exhausted by any one stratifying read of it — is the closer candidate for something plane-of-consistency-like at that scale, but establishing that precisely is further work this paper leaves open rather than claims to have done.

What would count against this reading, so it stays a claim rather than an escape from having one: if some future version of DMML's grammar forced convergence on `(subject, predicate)` pairs by default — a real "one winner" primitive, which does not exist today — the plane-of-consistency reading of the raw commit log would fail on its own terms, and this section's argument would need to be retracted, not patched. If a commit's `consumes` could not, as a matter of grammar, cite more than one prior fact at once, the double-articulation reading of recombination would have nothing to point at. Neither is the case in the grammar as it stands or in `pantheon.rs`'s own output, which is what makes the claim checkable rather than merely asserted — in keeping with what Section 3 already asks of every claim this paper makes, including this one.

This is not a proof that DMML's petitions satisfy Simondon's transindividual after all, arrived at by strata instead of individuation. Simondon named something real — the relation itself as the site of individuation — that this section does not discard so much as set aside as the wrong primary register: Deleuze and Guattari's own apparatus, developed independently and verified here against the primary text rather than secondary gloss, fits what DMML's grammar actually does — ongoing capture and release, never converging on a stable individual on either side — more precisely than a concept built to name a determinate outcome. Whether DMML's stratification behavior is best read as a distant cousin of Simondon's transindividual after all, or as something Deleuze and Guattari's own vocabulary was already reaching past it toward, is a real, open question this paper indicates a direction toward rather than settles. A petition, in these terms, is one particular case of the general pattern just argued for, not a separate mechanism: Section 1's door/`room/9` lifecycle is a first articulation (the petition selects the underdetermined node out of the wider field of everything a world could ask about) folded, by the resolving commit, into a second (a stable new room, available afterward to be cited, recombined, and further stratified exactly as `pantheon.rs`'s own facts are). That petitions needed no bespoke grammar primitive to fit this reading is not, by itself, evidence the reading is *right* — a description cheap enough to fit anything is thereby not distinctive of anything, the same caution the triad above exists to answer. What it does show, more modestly: this section's claims are not special-cased to petitions, engineered after the fact to make one particular mechanism look philosophically interesting. The same reading was built and checked against `pantheon.rs`'s ordinary, non-petition facts first; that a petition also fits it, without alteration, is consistent with the reading being about how DMML's commits work generally — consistent with, not proof of, since a genuinely general but wrong reading would fit both cases too.

## 5. The anti-Oedipal polemic does not transfer, and declining to force it is itself the argument

*Anti-Oedipus* is not a neutral metaphysics of production offered for general use. Its desiring-machines are introduced to dismantle a specific target: psychoanalysis's reduction of desire to the Oedipal family triangle, alongside capital's own reterritorialization of deterritorialized flows onto its own axiomatic. A world-modeling grammar has no unconscious to reduce to a family structure, and this paper does not manufacture a hidden anti-Oedipal argument where none exists.

What can be said honestly: DMML's closed, structural vocabulary contains no primitive representing lack, want, or need as foundational; anything of that shape has to be authored as ordinary, non-privileged, self-declared content, on the same footing as any other predicate. One genuine question sits adjacent to the polemic without being it: whether self-declared predicates, once introduced, tend over time to get absorbed into fixed convention across many authors and worlds — a small-scale, empirical version of the axiomatic-capture question — is real and cannot be answered without evidence about actual usage patterns across many worlds that does not yet exist. Declining to answer it here, rather than reaching for a plausible-sounding claim unsupported by data, is the same discipline the rest of this paper practices.

## 6. What this is not claiming

DMML is not offered as a psychoanalytic theory, a claim about human desire, or evidence of anything resembling consciousness. The correspondence argued here is structural and checkable at the level of the grammar (Section 3); extended to strata, deterritorialization, and the plane of consistency (Section 4), it is offered as a direction rather than a proof, checked against a real, runnable simulation rather than asserted, and stays honest about the difference. A production model borrowed at the level of one specific, load-bearing structural fact (no canonical-answer function) is a real correspondence worth stating precisely. It is not the same claim as inheriting either theory's full ontological weight.
