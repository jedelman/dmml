# DMML as a Desiring-Production Ontology

*Philosophy of technology / STS framing. Exact pincites for the Deleuze/Guattari and Simondon material are marked pending below and are needed before submission; the drafting history behind this text lives in this repository's own commit log and `dev-journal/`, not in the paper.*

## Abstract

DMML is a small declarative language for text-adventure world models: a `commit` block asserts what a world's state consumed and what it produced, and nothing else is a primitive. This paper argues DMML's commit model is a real, checkable structural correspondence to Deleuze and Guattari's desiring-production, not a decorative metaphor — and is equally careful about where that correspondence runs out. Its connectivity is rhizomatic rather than arborescent in Deleuze and Guattari's own specified sense, a claim that survives comparison not only to git and blockchain but to the open web and ordinary linked data, via a further, narrowly-stated property: DMML's edges cannot dangle, which the open web's can, though this is grounding rather than a guarantee of semantic fidelity, and multiplicity is what keeps a misreading from being silently promoted to canonical status in its place. Extending the analysis to Simondon's transindividual, we argue the right register for this extension is not a checklist of criteria to satisfy — treating individuation as a fixed, provable state is itself the arborescent move this paper's own argument warns against — but Deleuze and Guattari's own account of individuation without a stable subject, made specific to DMML's grammar rather than to any discrete event whatever: the declared, narrow territorialization of arbitrated consumption against a mergeable default, and connection across a sovereign identity boundary that the connection itself does not erase. Charge, on this reading, is not a property of a reconciled or unreconciled state; it inheres in the fact of change itself, what Deleuze and Guattari call speed. We indicate the direction this points rather than claim to have proven it arrives.

## 1. The mechanism, before any philosophy

A DMML world is a directed history of `commit` blocks. Each commit declares what it `consumes` — references to prior facts or nodes it depends on — and what it `produces` — new triples asserted into the world's graph. There is no primitive for editing a fact in place: an attribute change is itself a commit that consumes the old value and produces a new one, and the old commit's data stays physically in the history, only superseded for the purpose of reading current state. Retraction is the same shape with an empty `produces`.

A `commit`'s `consumes` entries are checked, at the point the commit is applied, for **referential integrity**: does the reference actually name something that was genuinely recorded — a real prior observation, not a fabricated one? This check does not verify that a reference is *current* (the most recent state of whatever it points to), and it does not verify that a new commit's `produces` is *semantically consistent* with what it consumes beyond citing something real. An older-but-genuinely-recorded reference is accepted, not rejected as stale. Two commits can cite the same real prior fact and produce contradictory claims about what follows from it, and the interpreter does not stop either one. What the system guarantees is that every citation in the history is real and permanent — provenance and non-repudiation — not that the history is free of contradiction.

A **petition** is a structured request raised against something underdetermined — a player asks what is behind a locked door, and no commit yet answers it — which an outside party (in practice, an AI acting as an author, operating under a persistent, signing identity rather than as a stateless one-off process) resolves. The resolution is itself a commit: it consumes the petition and produces the new fact, permanently linked back to the request. Nothing in DMML's grammar or interpreter computes an expected answer from the request; the request only establishes that some production is now outstanding, not what its content will be.

Concretely, one lifecycle: (1) a player commit `produces` a `Petition` node asking what is behind `door/1`; (2) an author commit `consumes` that petition and `produces` a new room, `room/9`, plus a fact linking `door/1` to it; (3) a later, unrelated commit — the player unlocking `door/1` — `consumes` the door's prior state and cites the room-linking fact from (2) as part of what it builds on. Step (3) is checked only for whether the fact it cites was genuinely produced in (2), not for whether (2)'s content was itself the "right" answer to (1)'s question, and not against whether some other commit has since produced a conflicting claim about `door/1`. That last gap is real and is what Section 4 turns on.

## 2. Desiring-machines, precisely

Deleuze and Guattari open *Anti-Oedipus* (1972; trans. Hurley, Seem, and Lane, 1977) with a claim introduced through examples before it is introduced through definition: "Everywhere it is machines — real ones, not figurative ones." The breast is a machine that produces milk; the mouth is a machine coupled to it, and immediately multifunctional — eating, speaking, breathing, a machine connecting into more than one production at once.<sup>[pincite pending, standard Minnesota Press edition, ch. 1 §1, "The Desiring-Machines"]</sup> The chapter's organizing thesis is that desire is not correctly modeled as lack seeking satisfaction: desire *produces*, and what it produces is real, not fantasy compensating for an absence.<sup>[same]</sup>

The phrase "real ones, not figurative ones" is doing specific rhetorical work in the source that should not be borrowed uncritically: it is aimed at desire's own self-understanding, insisting desire is not merely staged or theatrical, not a claim that any literal piece of software instantiates the thesis. What can be claimed instead, more modestly: DMML's primitives can be checked against the production-not-lack thesis as a structural constraint, and the check either passes or it doesn't. Section 3 does that check.

## 3. What the check actually shows, and what it doesn't

A system built on lack-and-satisfaction would need some representation of a deficit that a later event fills — a slot whose shape is fixed in advance, with the later event's job being to match it. DMML's grammar has no such primitive at the level of the language itself: nothing in the interpreter computes an expected or required `produces` from a `consumes`. That is a real, checkable fact about the grammar.

It is also narrower than "DMML is free of lack." Lack does not require a `LACK` predicate to be operative — it can live entirely in *content*: a locked door, a quest structure, a goal state a player is working toward, are all lack-shaped regardless of what the underlying grammar's primitives are. A world authored in DMML could be saturated with deficit-and-completion structure at the content layer while the grammar underneath stays clean of any built-in lack primitive. The claim that actually survives: **DMML's grammar does not impose lack-and-satisfaction as its own operative logic; it does not prevent, and has no opinion about, authors choosing to write lack-shaped content on top of it.** A claim about what the ontology requires, not about what every possible world built on it will do.

A second complication: the petition's resolution is, in this project's current implementation, produced by a large language model sampling from a learned distribution over its training data — reasonably describable as selection from an extremely large menu of already-determined possibilities, close to the very thing this section distinguishes DMML from. Whether sampling from a learned distribution counts as production in Deleuze and Guattari's sense is a separate, genuinely open question about generative models generally, not something DMML's own design settles. The claim this section actually makes is scoped narrower and stays real regardless: whatever the resolver's own internal process is, DMML's *grammar and interpreter* do not themselves compute a canonical target the resolver is checked against. The system-level absence of a built-in canonical-answer function is the claim; what happens inside whatever black box supplies the answer is out of scope.

One further concession: the claim above should be read as checkable, not falsifiable in a stronger sense — the only actual falsifying event would be changing the software itself, a design commitment stated in conditional form, not an empirical hypothesis exposed to independent test. What can be said is narrower and more useful: this is a claim about what the current grammar requires, offered so a reader can check it against the actual interpreter code rather than take it on the paper's word.

One piece of design history is the strongest evidence here and deserves to be stated plainly: an earlier version of this project's own AI-driven content generation computed petition-like answers from a fixed content model, and produced content that felt derivative — flatly, "content that just re-asserts something already true." The fix, arrived at from game-design concerns before any philosophical framing was applied to it, was removing exactly the canonical-answer computation this section argues against. That the diagnosis converges with Deleuze and Guattari's own critique of representational, selection-based models of desire is a small, independently-arrived-at instance of a critique they share with a broader anti-representational tradition — discovered by engineering failure, not derived from the theory, and useful evidence for exactly that reason.

## 4. Petitions, multiplicity, and the transindividual

Simondon's transindividual, developed across his doctoral thesis — the main part, *L'individuation à la lumière des notions de forme et d'information*, published 1964; a complementary part, *L'individuation psychique et collective*, published posthumously in 1989, one continuous argument split across two publication dates, not two independent works — is offered as a deliberate replacement for phenomenology's intersubjectivity: the relation itself is the site at which participants are individuated, not a channel between two already-formed individuals.<sup>[pincite pending; framing checked against the Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews treatment of Muriel Combes' *Gilbert Simondon and the Philosophy of the Transindividual*, MIT Press]</sup> The natural next move is to check a DMML petition against this concept criterion by criterion — metastability present or absent, the relation as site or merely registered afterward, both parties transformed or not, process ongoing or discrete — and report a verdict. That move should be resisted, not performed more carefully. Treating individuation as a fixed criterion a system either satisfies or fails is an arborescent way to argue: it assumes a stable, already-formed target concept that a candidate case either matches or doesn't, tracing a pre-given structure rather than mapping what is actually there. The substantive claim this section argues for is Deleuze and Guattari's own radicalization of Simondon — multiplicity, territorialization, and speed, rather than Simondon's criterion-set directly — as the register that actually fits what DMML's grammar does, not a fallback reached for after a criterion-by-criterion check turned out to be hard.

### The connectivity is rhizomatic, not arborescent

Deleuze and Guattari's "Introduction: Rhizome" (*A Thousand Plateaus*, 1980; trans. Massumi, 1987) opens the book, not incidentally, with multiplicity as a starting condition rather than a derived complication: "The two of us wrote *Anti-Oedipus* together. Since each of us was several, there was already quite a crowd."<sup>[content verified against the opening of "Introduction: Rhizome" via convergent secondary sourcing; print pincite pending]</sup> The chapter names, among six real principles, three directly relevant here: **connection and heterogeneity** — any point of a rhizome can and must connect to any other, unlike a tree's links, fixed by descent from one root; **multiplicity** — a rhizome has no unity to add to or subtract from, which a tree's branching, however complex, still lacks, since every branch traces back to one trunk; and **cartography over tracing** — a tree *traces*, reproducing a pre-given genealogical order; a rhizome *maps*, constructing rather than reproducing.

Checked against this, git and blockchain are not the right comparisons for DMML, and not equivalent to each other either. A blockchain is fairly arborescent without much qualification: Nakamoto consensus exists to collapse competing forks to one canonical chain, a hard principle of unity a rhizome's multiplicity refuses. Git is a closer call: its real commit history is a directed acyclic graph, not strictly a tree — merge commits have multiple parents, and nothing forces convergence, so forks can simply persist. What's tree-like about git is convention (the single "main" branch), not the underlying structure. DMML's own connectivity reduces to neither: a `consumes` entry can cite multiple prior facts anywhere in its history, `FactRef` addressing lets a commit cite one specific triple within any prior commit rather than only "the tip," and none of this is bounded to one repository the way even git's DAG is — a foreign-DID quotation lets a commit in one sovereign world cite a node from an entirely separate repository as a first-person percept, a connection across a boundary git's object graph has no mechanism for referencing without importing (a submodule copies; it does not live-reference). Where a blockchain collapses competing claims to one winner, DMML's own consume-kind default is *mergeable*: multiple concurrent observations of the same fact coexist permanently, with no forced resolution, and only a narrow, explicitly-declared class of one-in-one-out resources — petitions among them — is *arbitrated* at all. Two independent identities quoting the same third party can disagree permanently and correctly, by design.

Git and blockchain are not the hardest comparison, though. The hyperlinked web satisfies connection/heterogeneity, multiplicity, and cross-boundary linking about as well as DMML does; so does the Semantic Web's own RDF/linked-data model, which DMML's own subject-predicate-object graph structurally resembles; so does an academic citation network. If rhizomatic topology alone were what this argument needed, the open web would already be individuating at planetary scale, which is not a conclusion worth defending. One real property DMML's mechanism does add, though it needs to be stated at the strength it actually has and no further: a DMML `consumes` reference cannot dangle — the referential-integrity check (Section 1) rejects a citation unless the thing cited was genuinely, verifiably recorded. A hyperlink or an ordinary citation carries no such guarantee; it can point at nothing, or at something that never said what the pointer implies. This is grounding, not semantic fidelity, and the difference matters: the check does not verify that what a commit *produces* stays faithful to what it *consumes* — a commit can cite a real fact and build something on it that misreads or misrepresents it, and nothing in the grammar catches that. What keeps this from collapsing the whole distinction is the same default already named above: because resolution is *mergeable* rather than forced, a misreading does not get silently promoted to canonical status the way an unchecked hyperlink effectively can when nothing else contests it — it persists instead as one more citable, disputable production alongside whatever contests it, findable and arguable rather than laundered into an accepted fact by default. The rhizome here is not one where every edge is beyond dispute. It is one where disputes themselves are forced to stay structurally present rather than resolved by default — multiplicity preserved at the level of contested claims, not innocence purchased at the level of individual edges.

### Individuation without a stable self

Here is where a checklist against Simondon's criteria would ordinarily continue, and where this paper instead follows Deleuze and Guattari's own move past him — a real, scholarship-attested move, not this paper's own conflation of two different thinkers. Deleuze and Guattari draw on Simondon directly (Deleuze's own 1966 review of him, and citations across *Difference and Repetition* and *Logic of Sense*, with Simondon cited directly in *A Thousand Plateaus* as well), but the relationship is extension, not restatement. One source states the relationship for Deleuze and Guattari specifically: they "pick up Simondon's critique of hylomorphism and provide an account of an energetic, vitalist materialism — a move going beyond Simondon's ideas."<sup>[pincite pending, secondary source; corroborated by two papers organized around exactly this distinction, *"Singular without being individual": Simondon, Deleuze and the Problem of Individuation* and *Differences in Becoming: Gilbert Simondon and Gilles Deleuze on Individuation* — author names and full pincites pending]</sup> Simondon's individuation still names a determinate process with a real, locatable outcome; Deleuze and Guattari push further, toward treating even that outcome as one coding among others rather than a resting point. For Deleuze and Guattari, a stable, bounded, self-identical individual is not simply given — it is closer to an effect of *territorialization*: a coding, a stabilization, imposed on something that is actually stranger and more mobile, a field of flows and intensities they call the molecular, as against the fixed, coded "molar" identities that appear to sit still on top of it.<sup>[pincite pending; molar/molecular distinction, *Anti-Oedipus* and *A Thousand Plateaus* throughout]</sup>

This is not merely a change of vocabulary; it names something DMML's own grammar actually distinguishes structurally, not just an event that happened to occur inside it. The *mergeable* default, named above as the connectivity subsection's own point of contrast with blockchain's forced convergence — permanent coexistence of rival claims, no forced resolution — is deterritorialization built into the grammar as the ordinary case: nothing about DMML routes a fact toward becoming the one stable, coded answer unless a world's author specifically asks for that. *Arbitrated* consumption, the narrow, explicitly-declared class petitions belong to, is the opposite: a deliberate, local act of territorialization, a request that this one register a single, forced-convergent answer rather than a standing multiplicity. Most systems that record events do not make this distinction available at all — an ordinary database transaction, an HTTP request, a generic git commit have no structural notion of "this fact is allowed to stay contested" versus "this fact must resolve to one." DMML's grammar does, and a petition sits specifically on the territorializing side of that distinction: a place where the grammar itself asks for one coded answer, inside a system whose default is otherwise the opposite. Cross-DID quotation adds a second, independent axis: a molar boundary — one sovereign, signing identity's own repository — is exactly the kind of coded, bounded unit Deleuze and Guattari mean by the molar, and a foreign-DID citation is a connection that crosses that boundary without erasing it, without merging the two repositories into one authority. Neither of these — the arbitrated/mergeable split, or cross-sovereign quotation across an identity boundary — is a property any discrete committed event has merely by being discrete, singular, and irreversible; they are properties of how DMML's grammar specifically organizes territorialization and connection, which is the actual, non-generic target this section needs.

Deleuze and Guattari also give a name to individuation that requires no subject and no substance at all: **haecceity**. "A season, a winter, a summer, an hour, a date have a perfect individuality lacking nothing, even though this individuality is different from that of a thing or a subject."<sup>[verified near-verbatim across independent sources; "1730: Becoming-Intense, Becoming-Animal, Becoming-Imperceptible...", print pincite pending]</sup> A haecceity is individuated by its own **longitude and latitude** — the relations of speed and slowness among its parts, its capacity to affect and be affected — not by being an instance of a fixed kind or a persisting self.<sup>[verified against "Memories of a Spinozist I & II", same plateau; print pincite pending]</sup> The concept fits a petition's resolution *only once it is specified by the structural facts just named*, not by discreteness and irreversibility alone — those hold of any committed event anywhere and would prove too much on their own, a failure this section's own rhizome argument already had to correct for once. What is specific to a petition's resolution is that it is a haecceity produced by crossing from the mergeable side of the grammar to the arbitrated side, at a moment declared as a request rather than defaulting there, and that it stands available afterward to be cited across sovereign boundaries by identities other than the two that produced it. That is a configuration DMML's grammar makes structurally visible, not a description that would apply identically to a database write.

What would count against this reading, so it stays a claim rather than an escape from having one: if DMML's grammar made *arbitrated* the default and *mergeable* the narrow exception, the territorialization/deterritorialization structure just described would run backwards, and this argument would fail on its own terms. If cross-DID quotation silently collapsed distinct sovereign repositories into one shared canonical record rather than preserving the boundary it crosses, the molar/molecular reading above would fail the same way. Neither is the case in the grammar as it stands, which is what makes the claim checkable rather than merely asserted — in keeping with what Section 3 already asks of every claim this paper makes, including this one.

This is not offered as a proof that DMML's petitions satisfy Simondon's transindividual after all, arrived at by a better route than the checklist. Simondon named real phenomena — metastability, relation-as-site, ongoing process — that a fixed-criteria check kept either finding thin or finding nowhere at all, because those criteria assume the very kind of stable, bounded individual Deleuze and Guattari's own multiplicity already puts in question. What can be said without overreaching: a petition's resolution is a haecceity in the specific, structural sense argued above — individuated by its position in DMML's territorializing/deterritorializing grammar and by the sovereign boundary it crosses, not merely by being one more discrete, irreversible event. Whether that configuration is transindividual in Simondon's fuller sense, or is instead the more radical alternative Deleuze and Guattari were already offering in its place, is a real, open question this paper indicates a direction toward rather than settles.

## 5. The anti-Oedipal polemic does not transfer, and declining to force it is itself the argument

*Anti-Oedipus* is not a neutral metaphysics of production offered for general use. Its desiring-machines are introduced to dismantle a specific target: psychoanalysis's reduction of desire to the Oedipal family triangle, alongside capital's own reterritorialization of deterritorialized flows onto its own axiomatic. A world-modeling grammar has no unconscious to reduce to a family structure, and this paper does not manufacture a hidden anti-Oedipal argument where none exists.

What can be said honestly: DMML's closed, structural vocabulary contains no primitive representing lack, want, or need as foundational; anything of that shape has to be authored as ordinary, non-privileged, self-declared content, on the same footing as any other predicate. One genuine question sits adjacent to the polemic without being it: whether self-declared predicates, once introduced, tend over time to get absorbed into fixed convention across many authors and worlds — a small-scale, empirical version of the axiomatic-capture question — is real and cannot be answered without evidence about actual usage patterns across many worlds that does not yet exist. Declining to answer it here, rather than reaching for a plausible-sounding claim unsupported by data, is the same discipline the rest of this paper practices.

## 6. What this is not claiming

DMML is not offered as a psychoanalytic theory, a claim about human desire, or evidence of anything resembling consciousness. The correspondence argued here is structural and checkable at the level of the grammar (Section 3); extended to individuation (Section 4), it is offered as a direction rather than a proof, and stays honest about the difference. A production model borrowed at the level of one specific, load-bearing structural fact (no canonical-answer function) is a real correspondence worth stating precisely. It is not the same claim as inheriting either theory's full ontological weight.
