# DMML as a Desiring-Production Ontology

## Abstract

DMML is a small declarative language for text-adventure world models: a `commit` block asserts what a world's state consumed and what it produced, and nothing else is a primitive. This paper argues DMML's commit model is a real, checkable structural correspondence to Deleuze and Guattari's desiring-production, not a decorative metaphor — and is equally careful about where that correspondence runs out. Extending the analysis to Simondon's transindividual, we argue DMML's connectivity is rhizomatic rather than arborescent in Deleuze and Guattari's own specified sense, and that this survives comparison not only to git and blockchain but to the open web and ordinary linked data, via a further property: DMML's connections are verified productions, not claims that could be figurative. We report two further, harder attempts to locate Simondonian charge and individuation in the system's grammar and runtime respectively, and why neither survived scrutiny — one analogy runs in the wrong direction, one rests on a mechanical claim the software does not actually support. We treat the failure of these attempts as real information about where the correspondence's limit actually sits, not as something to omit in favor of a cleaner-sounding result.

## 1. The mechanism, before any philosophy

A DMML world is a directed history of `commit` blocks. Each commit declares what it `consumes` — references to prior facts or nodes it depends on — and what it `produces` — new triples asserted into the world's graph. There is no primitive for editing a fact in place: an attribute change is itself a commit that consumes the old value and produces a new one, and the old commit's data stays physically in the history, only superseded for the purpose of reading current state. Retraction is the same shape with an empty `produces`.

A `commit`'s `consumes` entries are checked, at the point the commit is applied, for **referential integrity**: does the reference actually name something that was genuinely recorded — a real prior observation, not a fabricated one? What this check does *not* do is worth stating precisely, because a later section of this paper once got it wrong: it does not verify that a reference is *current* (the most recent state of whatever it points to), and it does not verify that a new commit's `produces` is *semantically consistent* with what it consumes beyond citing something real. An older-but-genuinely-recorded reference is accepted, not rejected as stale. Two commits can cite the same real prior fact and produce contradictory claims about what follows from it, and the interpreter does not stop either one. What the system guarantees is that every citation in the history is real and permanent — provenance and non-repudiation — not that the history is free of contradiction.

A **petition** is a structured request raised against something underdetermined — a player asks what is behind a locked door, and no commit yet answers it — which an outside party (in practice, an AI acting as an author, operating under a persistent, signing identity rather than as a stateless one-off process) resolves. The resolution is itself a commit: it consumes the petition and produces the new fact, permanently linked back to the request. Nothing in DMML's grammar or interpreter computes an expected answer from the request; the request only establishes that some production is now outstanding, not what its content will be.

Concretely, one lifecycle: (1) a player commit `produces` a `Petition` node asking what is behind `door/1`; (2) an author commit `consumes` that petition and `produces` a new room, `room/9`, plus a fact linking `door/1` to it; (3) a later, unrelated commit — the player unlocking `door/1` — `consumes` the door's prior state and cites the room-linking fact from (2) as part of what it builds on. Step (3) is checked only for whether the fact it cites was genuinely produced in (2), not for whether (2)'s content was itself the "right" answer to (1)'s question, and not against whether some other commit has since produced a conflicting claim about `door/1`. That last gap is real and is what Section 4 below turns on.

## 2. Desiring-machines, precisely

Deleuze and Guattari open *Anti-Oedipus* (1972; trans. Hurley, Seem, and Lane, 1977) with a claim introduced through examples before it is introduced through definition: "Everywhere it is machines — real ones, not figurative ones." The breast is a machine that produces milk; the mouth is a machine coupled to it, and immediately multifunctional — eating, speaking, breathing, a machine connecting into more than one production at once.<sup>[pincite pending, standard Minnesota Press edition, ch. 1 §1, "The Desiring-Machines"]</sup> The chapter's organizing thesis is that desire is not correctly modeled as lack seeking satisfaction: desire *produces*, and what it produces is real, not fantasy compensating for an absence.<sup>[same]</sup>

The phrase "real ones, not figurative ones" is doing specific rhetorical work in the source that this paper should not borrow uncritically: it is aimed at desire's own self-understanding, insisting desire is not merely staged or theatrical, not a claim that any literal piece of software instantiates the thesis. Quoting it to license "our system is the thesis, literally" would misuse it. What can be claimed instead, more modestly: DMML's primitives can be checked against the production-not-lack thesis as a structural constraint, and the check either passes or it doesn't. Section 3 does that check.

## 3. What the check actually shows, and what it doesn't

A system built on lack-and-satisfaction would need some representation of a deficit that a later event fills — a slot whose shape is fixed in advance, with the later event's job being to match it. DMML's grammar has no such primitive at the level of the language itself: nothing in the interpreter computes an expected or required `produces` from a `consumes`. That is a real, checkable fact about the grammar.

It is also a narrower fact than "DMML is free of lack" would be, and the earlier version of this argument did not say so plainly enough. Lack does not require a `LACK` predicate to be operative — it can live entirely in *content*: a locked door, a quest structure, a goal state a player is working toward, are all lack-shaped regardless of what the underlying grammar's primitives are. A world authored in DMML could be saturated with deficit-and-completion structure at the content layer while the grammar underneath stays clean of any built-in lack primitive. So the claim this section can actually defend is: **DMML's grammar does not impose lack-and-satisfaction as its own operative logic; it does not prevent, and has no opinion about, authors choosing to write lack-shaped content on top of it.** That is a claim about what the ontology requires, not about what every possible world built on it will do — and stated that way, it survives the objection rather than pretending the objection doesn't apply.

A second honest complication, not resolved here: the petition's resolution is, in this project's current implementation, produced by a large language model sampling from a learned distribution over its training data. One could reasonably describe that as selection from an extremely large menu of already-determined possibilities, which is close to the very thing Section 3's argument distinguishes DMML from. This paper does not resolve whether sampling from a learned distribution counts as production in Deleuze and Guattari's sense — that is a separate, genuinely open question about generative models generally, not something DMML's own design settles. What this paper's claim is scoped to is narrower and still real: whatever the resolver's own internal process is, DMML's *grammar and interpreter* do not themselves compute a canonical target the resolver is checked against. The system-level absence of a built-in canonical-answer function is the claim; what happens inside whatever black box supplies the answer is out of scope.

One further concession, made directly rather than left for a reviewer to find: the earlier framing of this argument reached for Popperian falsifiability language ("if DMML added a canonical-answer function, the claim would be refuted"). On reflection that framing borrows more authority than it earns — the only actual falsifying event would be the authors changing their own software, which is a design commitment stated in conditional form, not an empirical hypothesis exposed to independent test. The honest version: this is a claim about what the current grammar requires, offered so that a reader can check it against the actual interpreter code rather than take it on the paper's word — checkability, not falsifiability in the stronger sense.

There is one real, independently motivated piece of design history worth reporting plainly, because it is the paper's best evidence and deserves more confidence than a first draft gave it: an earlier version of this project's own AI-driven content generation computed petition-like answers from a fixed content model, and produced content that felt derivative — flatly, "content that just re-asserts something already true." The fix, arrived at from game-design concerns before any of this paper's philosophical framing was applied to it, was removing exactly the canonical-answer computation Section 3 argues against. That the diagnosis converges with Deleuze and Guattari's own critique of representational, selection-based models of desire is worth stating as what it is: a small, independently-arrived-at instance of a critique they share with a broader anti-representational tradition, discovered by engineering failure rather than derived from the theory, and evidentially useful for exactly that reason.

## 4. Petitions and the transindividual — what actually holds

Simondon's transindividual, laid out at length in *L'individuation psychique et collective* (1989) as part of a continuous argument that also spans *L'individuation à la lumière des notions de forme et d'information* — the two are not independent works and should not be cited as though the transindividual belongs to one and metastability to the other — is offered as a deliberate replacement for phenomenology's intersubjectivity. Reading the Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews treatment of Muriel Combes' *Gilbert Simondon and the Philosophy of the Transindividual* (MIT Press), the concept's distinctive content is not "two parties producing something together" (ordinary collaboration already does that) but something stronger: the relation itself is the site at which the participants are individuated, not a channel between two already-formed individuals.

An earlier, less formal essay from this project reached for this concept to describe the relation between a petitioning player and a resolving author, and its own review correctly downgraded an overclaim ("formally, not by analogy") to a hedge ("structurally, not by full derivation"). A first attempt at a harder version of this section conceded the ground entirely, on the reasoning that any append-only, integrity-checked log — a git repository, a blockchain — satisfies the same structural facts DMML does, so nothing about DMML's own design was doing the individuating work. That concession itself needs to be checked rather than taken as settled, because it quietly assumed all persistent, checkable histories have the same *shape*, and Deleuze and Guattari — writing after *Anti-Oedipus*, in *A Thousand Plateaus* (1980; trans. Massumi, 1987) — spend an entire chapter arguing that persistent structures do not all have the same shape, and that the difference matters.

### Arborescent vs. rhizomatic: the comparison class was wrong, not the objection

"Introduction: Rhizome" names, among other things, three principles directly relevant here.<sup>[D&G actually name six: connection, heterogeneity, multiplicity, asignifying rupture, cartography, and decalcomania — the three below are the ones this section draws on, not the full set]</sup> **Connection and heterogeneity**: "any point of a rhizome can be connected to anything other, and must be" — unlike a tree, where a given node's links are fixed by its position in a hierarchy descending from one root. **Multiplicity**: a rhizome has no unity to be added to or subtracted from; it is a multiplicity as such, which a tree's branching structure — however complex — is not, because a tree's branches all still trace back to one trunk. **Cartography over tracing**: a tree structure *traces* — reproduces — a pre-given genealogical order (the tree of knowledge, the family tree); a rhizome *maps*, constructing rather than reproducing, with "no genetic axis" fixing what connects to what in advance.

Checked against this, not just against "is there a permanent log," git and blockchain are not equivalent cases, and neither is straightforwardly the right comparison for DMML. A blockchain is fairly described as arborescent without much qualification: Nakamoto consensus exists specifically to collapse competing forks down to one canonical chain — a hard, structural principle of unity that a rhizome's multiplicity explicitly refuses. Git is a closer call, and the honest version matters: git's real commit history is a directed acyclic graph, not strictly a tree — a merge commit has multiple parents, and git has no consensus mechanism forcing convergence the way a blockchain does, so forks can simply persist. What is tree-*like* about git is convention, not the underlying data structure: the ordinary, non-merge commit relation is single-parent, and the "main"/trunk branch is a near-universal social workflow layered on top of a more general graph. Git is a DAG with a strong single-trunk convention, not a tree; a blockchain is much closer to an actual tree, by design.

DMML's own connectivity does not reduce to either. A `consumes` entry is not a parent pointer restricted to one immediate predecessor — a commit can cite multiple prior facts anywhere in its own history, and `FactRef` addressing lets a commit cite one specific triple within any prior commit, not only "the tip." More importantly, none of this is bounded to one repository the way even git's multi-parent DAG is: a foreign-DID quotation lets a commit in one sovereign world cite a node from an entirely separate, unrelated repository as a first-person percept — a connection across a boundary git's own object graph has no mechanism for crossing at all. And where a blockchain's whole design collapses simultaneous, competing claims to one winner, DMML's own consume-kind default (documented in this project's own multi-tenant substrate design notes) is *mergeable*: multiple concurrent observations of the same fact coexist permanently, with no forced resolution, and only a narrow, explicitly-declared class of one-in-one-out resources — petitions among them — is *arbitrated* at all. Two independent identities quoting the same third party can disagree permanently and correctly, by design, which is close to a direct implementation of "no unity to be added to or subtracted from" rather than a resemblance to it. This is, as far as a real search could confirm, not a comparison already made in the published literature applying D&G's rhizome/tree distinction to version control or content-addressed data structures specifically — treated here as an honest gap in what a web-accessible search could reach, not a confirmed claim to novelty, and at least one non-academic source has already made the narrower blockchain-is-arborescent move using the same framework, which this paper does not claim to be first to notice.

### The comparison class is still too narrow, and there is a real answer, not just a bigger hedge

Git and blockchain are not the only, or even the hardest, comparison. Connection and heterogeneity, sustained multiplicity, cross-boundary linking with no forced convergence — the hyperlinked web satisfies all three about as well as DMML does; so does the Semantic Web's own RDF/linked-data model, which DMML's own subject-predicate-object graph structurally resembles; so does an academic citation network. If rhizomatic topology alone were what the argument needed, the conclusion would have to be that the open web is already doing Simondonian individuation at planetary scale, which is not a conclusion this paper is prepared to defend, and reaching it would mean the comparison class was still drawn too narrowly — exactly the mistake corrected once already in this section.

What the web, ordinary RDF, and citation networks share, and DMML's actual mechanism does not, is that a *connection can lie*. A hyperlink can point at a page that no longer exists or never said what the link implied; a citation can misrepresent a source with no structural check preventing it; an RDF triple's object can dereference to nothing, or to something altered since the triple was written. A DMML `consumes` reference cannot do this in the same way: the issue #53 referential-integrity check rejects a citation unless the thing cited was genuinely, verifiably produced — a fabricated or merely-claimed connection is not admitted as a connection at all. This is not a difference of degree in how richly connected the graph is; it is a difference in whether a given edge in the graph is a *real* relation or a *represented* one, and it recovers, at the level of the connective tissue itself, the distinction Section 2 opened with: "everywhere it is machines — real ones, not figurative ones." An open, unverified rhizome (the web, ordinary linked data, citation practice) is still, in this specific sense, a rhizome of claims that could be figurative. DMML's is a rhizome in which every edge is, by construction, non-figurative — verified production, not asserted reference. Whether that specific difference is what Simondon's own individuation-through-relation actually requires (a relation that only counts if it is not merely claimed but genuinely, checkably real) is argued here as a plausible extension of the concept, not confirmed against Simondon's own text, which does not address verification or fabrication risk at all — a real limit on how far this paragraph's own move can be pushed, named rather than glossed over.

### What this actually buys the Simondon argument, and what it doesn't

Blocking the "any append-only log does this" objection is not the same as showing DMML's petition mechanism satisfies Simondon's transindividual. It removes a bad reason to dismiss the comparison; it does not by itself supply a good reason to accept it. Checked component by component, against what scholarship (via the Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews treatment of Muriel Combes' *Gilbert Simondon and the Philosophy of the Transindividual*) treats as the concept's real content — the relation itself as the site of individuation, not a channel between two already-formed parties:

- **Metastability.** *Two attempted relocations, both tried and rejected on review — reported here rather than erased, because the reasons they fail are informative.* A first attempt located charge in the graph's own state (permanent non-reconciliation as held tension); flagged, correctly, as unable to distinguish charge from mere stasis. A second attempt relocated charge to desire's own movement through the grammar, via Deleuze and Guattari's real, D&G-stated link between deterritorialization and *agrammatical* expression (*A Thousand Plateaus*, "Postulates of Linguistics" — an agrammatical expression is explicitly called "a cutting edge of deterritorialization of language"), applied to DMML by analogy: a new self-declared predicate entering the open vocabulary, read as agrammatical, expanding the grammar rather than being generated by it. This analogy does not survive scrutiny: an agrammatical expression works because it violates a constraint the language-user is otherwise bound by — Cummings' "he danced his did" pushes against real resistance. DMML's self-declaration mechanism is the opposite of transgressive: it is a sanctioned extensibility feature the grammar itself defines and expects, with no limit being pushed past. Worse, if routine self-declaration counted as agrammatical deterritorialization, ordinary linked-data namespacing (anyone minting a new predicate URI) would equally count, reopening exactly the wide-comparison-class problem the previous section fought to close. This component is left where the state-based attempt left it: real, thin, and unresolved — a single named gap per petition, not a charged field, with no stronger reading that survived checking.
- **The relation as the site of individuation, not a channel registered afterward.** *One relocation attempted and rejected on review, for a reason worth keeping visible: it rested on a factual error about the software.* The persistent citation graph is the wrong place to look — anything read there is, by construction, read after the resolving commit already exists. An attempt to relocate the site to `apply_commit`'s own runtime execution, reasoning that a petition's indeterminacy resolves live at that moment, does not hold: per Section 1, a resolution's `produces` content is authored — written into the commit — before `apply_commit` ever runs; the interpreter checks referential integrity and applies an already-determinate declaration, it does not generate one. Indeterminacy resolves at authoring time, not at application time, which relocates the problem rather than escaping it — and relocating to authoring time instead runs directly into Section 3's own concession that the resolver's internal process (an LLM sampling from a learned distribution) is exactly what this paper declined to characterize as production or not. This remains the hardest component and the one this paper does not claim to have closed by any route tried so far: a resolution's meaning stays open to future connection (Section 4's rhizome argument), but the connections that keep it open all happen after the resolving commit exists, and no candidate site checked — the graph, the runtime event, or authorship itself — has been shown to be where the relation, rather than its trace, actually does the individuating.
- **Both relata transformed.** *Real, and arguably richer under a rhizomatic reading than a dyadic one.* The player's and the resolving identity's own future commits are constrained, as Draft 2 argued. Because the connection structure is not bounded to the two original parties, though, the relation's effects are not confined to a dyad the way a private, two-party ledger entry's would be — any other identity that later quotes or cites the resolution is drawn into a structure the original two parties created, which is closer to Simondon's transindividual exceeding the pair than a simple two-party constraint is. This remains a claim about accumulating, structurally open constraint on future action, not about either party's identity being transformed in Simondon's fuller sense.
- **Process character, not a discrete completed event.** *Real change from Draft 2's assessment, git corrected out of the comparison.* A single petition's resolution commit is still, in itself, a discrete, terminal write — and git is the wrong contrast to make that point against, since a git commit is never actually sealed either: any clone can extend any historical commit indefinitely, and a merge finalizes nothing at the level of the underlying DAG. The real contrast is a blockchain's finalized block, whose transactions are done, by protocol design, once sufficient confirmation has passed. DMML's own resolution commit is closer to git's case than to a block's: nothing finalizes the set of who may still connect to it. What is different from git is not finality but *default expectation* — git's single-trunk convention treats a merged branch as closed in practice even though the DAG permits otherwise; DMML's mergeable-by-default design treats continued, non-converging connection as the normal case, not an edge case fighting the system's own grain. The individuating process, read at the level of the graph rather than the single commit, does not terminate the way a blockchain's does and is not merely tolerated the way a rarely-exercised git capability is. This is the strongest of the four components under the rhizomatic reading, though "strongest" here means most consistent with rhizomatic connectivity, not confirmed against Simondon's own further requirement that the process be ongoing in some richer sense than "technically still extensible."

Put precisely, after this revision: two of the four components (both relata; process character) hold up as real, qualified upgrades from the rhizome argument in the subsection above — constraint propagation reaching past the original dyad, and non-termination in a way a blockchain's finality or git's own social convention doesn't share. The other two — metastability and relation-as-site — were each pushed harder in this round, twice over in relation-as-site's case, and neither move survived adversarial review; both are reported at the honest place they were left rather than the more flattering place a further reframing might have reached. This asymmetry is itself informative rather than a failure of nerve: it is easier to show DMML's connectivity structure genuinely differs from git, blockchain, and open linked data (a real, checkable, topological and epistemic claim) than it is to show that structure individuates its participants in Simondon's fuller sense (a claim about what a relation does to its relata, which this paper tried three separate ways to locate — the graph, the grammar, the runtime event — and could not honestly close on any of them). What can be said with real confidence: the comparison to Simondon's transindividual survives every objection raised against its *connectivity* claim (the shape of a persistent log, the shape of an open linked-data graph) without weakening it to escape them. It does not yet survive as a claim about individuation of the parties themselves, and this paper's own repeated, failed attempts to make that further claim are better evidence of the limit than a single hedge would have been.

## 5. The anti-Oedipal polemic does not transfer, and declining to force it is itself the argument

*Anti-Oedipus* is not a neutral metaphysics of production offered for general use. Its desiring-machines are introduced to dismantle a specific target: psychoanalysis's reduction of desire to the Oedipal family triangle, alongside capital's own reterritorialization of deterritorialized flows onto its own axiomatic. A world-modeling grammar has no unconscious to reduce to a family structure, and this paper does not manufacture a hidden anti-Oedipal argument where none exists.

What can be said honestly: DMML's closed, structural vocabulary contains no primitive representing lack, want, or need as foundational; anything of that shape has to be authored as ordinary, non-privileged, self-declared content, on the same footing as any other predicate. One genuine question sits adjacent to the polemic without being it, and is named here as future work rather than claimed now: whether self-declared predicates, once introduced, tend over time to get absorbed into fixed convention across many authors and worlds — a small-scale, empirical version of the axiomatic-capture question — is a real question this paper cannot answer without evidence about actual usage patterns across many worlds that does not yet exist. Declining to answer it here, rather than reaching for a plausible-sounding claim unsupported by data, is the same discipline the rest of this paper tries to practice.

## 6. What this is not claiming

DMML is not offered as a psychoanalytic theory, a claim about human desire, or evidence of anything resembling consciousness. The correspondence argued here is structural, checkable at the level of the grammar (Section 3), and — per Section 4's own finding — only partially sustained once tested against Simondon's full criterion. A production model borrowed at the level of one specific, load-bearing structural fact (no canonical-answer function) is a real correspondence worth stating precisely. It is not the same claim as inheriting either theory's full ontological weight, and where this paper's own checking showed that weight isn't earned, it says so rather than reaching for looser language to cover the gap.
