# Citation Verification Pass — Rhizome / Arborescent (2026-08-24, follow-on)

This is a new, follow-on verification pass, distinct from the same-day
`CITATION-VERIFICATION-2026-08-24.md`, which covered *Anti-Oedipus* and
Simondon citations for `desiring-production-ontology/DRAFT.md`. That pass did
not touch Deleuze and Guattari's rhizome/arborescent distinction from *A
Thousand Plateaus* (1980; trans. Massumi, 1987) — the author now wants to
bring this concept into the paper (likely as an extension to Section 4's
argument about DMML's commit-graph structure), and it has not been checked
before. Grades use the project's standard scale: ✅ Verified · ⚠️ Plausible,
unverified · ❌ Wrong · 🚫 Unverifiable · ☠️ Fabricated.

## Claim 1 — "Introduction: Rhizome" opens with arborescent-vs-rhizomatic contrast; real, specific named principles

**Claim:** D&G's "Introduction: Rhizome" contrasts arborescent (tree-like,
single-root, hierarchical, binary-branching) structures with rhizomatic ones,
and names real, specific principles of the rhizome, commonly summarized as
connection and heterogeneity, multiplicity, and asignifying rupture.

**Grade: ✅ Verified — with one correction: there are six named principles, not three**

**Sources found:**
- `interglacial.com`'s page on the rhizome, explicitly quoting the Brian
  Massumi translation, gives direct textual quotes matching the claim almost
  verbatim: "any point of a rhizome can be connected to anything other, and
  must be" (connection/heterogeneity); "Multiplicities are rhizomatic, and
  expose arborescent pseudomultiplicities for what they are" (multiplicity);
  "A rhizome may be broken, shattered at a given spot, but it will start up
  again on one of its old lines" (asignifying rupture).
- `csmt.uchicago.edu`'s annotation page, citing page numbers from the
  Massumi edition, independently corroborates: "each point is necessarily
  connected to each other point... yet the whole is heterogeneous" (p.7
  area), the rhizome as "multiplicity" not "multiple," "We're tired of
  trees. We should stop believing in trees, roots, and radicles" (p.15),
  and "the rhizome is an antigenealogy" (p.21).
- Multiple independent secondary sources (Then & Now essay,
  literarysphere.com's "Principles of Rhizome" summary, In the Ravine blog)
  converge on the same count: **the rhizome chapter names six principles**,
  not three: (1) connection, (2) heterogeneity — usually treated as one
  combined principle in secondary summaries, matching the outline's framing
  — (3) multiplicity, (4) asignifying rupture, (5) cartography, and (6)
  decalcomania. The outline's summary picks out three of the six real ones
  (connection/heterogeneity as one, multiplicity, asignifying rupture) and
  states them accurately, but presents them as if that's the full list. It
  isn't — cartography and decalcomania are the other two, and they're
  closely tied to the tracing/map distinction covered in Claim 2.

**Assessment:** The three principles the claim describes are real and
accurately summarized against the actual text. The claim's framing implies
these are the complete set (or is silent on the count), which is the one
correction needed: the paper should either name all six or explicitly say
"among the six principles D&G name, the three most relevant here are..."
rather than imply three is the total.

## Claim 2 — Tracing vs. mapping: trees have single root/unity/hierarchy and "trace" a pre-given structure; rhizomes "map"

**Claim:** D&G contrast rhizome with genealogical/tree-structured models
specifically because trees have a single root and a principle of
unity/hierarchy that *traces* a pre-given structure, while a rhizome *maps*
and has no genetic axis or deep structure.

**Grade: ✅ Verified**

**Sources found:**
- Same two sources as Claim 1. `csmt.uchicago.edu`: "What distinguishes the
  map from the tracing is that it is entirely oriented toward an
  experimentation in contact with the real" (p.12 area) — maps construct new
  possibilities, tracings reproduce existing closed systems.
- `interglacial.com`: "The rhizome is altogether different, a map and not a
  tracing. Make a map, not a tracing" — direct Massumi-translation quote.
- `csmt.uchicago.edu` also gives the genealogical framing directly: "There
  is always something genealogical about a tree... the rhizome is an
  antigenealogy" (p.21), and "We're tired of trees. We should stop believing
  in trees, roots, and radicles" (p.15).
- A prior independent search summary (not fetched verbatim but consistent
  with the above two primary-adjacent sources) states the same point: "The
  arborescent model reproduces a structure that is taken to be prior — it
  traces the tree of knowledge, the tree of life, the family tree, the
  organizational chart. The rhizome, by contrast, is always making a map."

**Assessment:** This is a real, well-attested distinction, one of D&G's own
named principles (cartography/decalcomania, per Claim 1's correction — the
"tracing vs. map" material is not a separate freestanding idea but part of
that principle). The claim's summary is accurate.

## Claim 3 — Has this rhizome/arborescent distinction already been applied to version control, blockchain, or content-addressed/append-only data structures in existing academic literature?

**Grade: 🚫 Nothing found in academic literature — but flag one adjacent, non-academic source the paper should be aware of, and be honest about search limits**

**What was searched:** Multiple queries combining "rhizome," "arborescent,"
"Deleuze," "git," "version control," "blockchain," "distributed ledger,"
"content-addressed," and "Merkle tree" across general web search, plus
targeted checks of Google Scholar-adjacent results (ResearchGate, PhilPapers,
arXiv, Semantic Scholar-indexed pages that surfaced).

**What was found:**
- **No academic (peer-reviewed, published, or clearly citable) source** was
  located that applies D&G's rhizome/arborescent distinction specifically to
  git, version control, distributed ledgers, or content-addressed/append-only
  data structures. Searches for "rhizome" + "Merkle"/"content-addressed"/
  "distributed ledger" returned only tangential hits (education/posthumanism
  applications of rhizome theory, unrelated).
- **One non-academic source is close enough to be worth naming so the paper
  doesn't accidentally duplicate it uncredited**: `jzhao.xyz/thoughts/
  rhizomatic-vs-arborescent`, a personal blog/"digital garden" note (Jacky
  Zhao, dated 2022-08-02), which does explicitly write: "The structure of a
  blockchain follows this very closely" in a section characterizing
  arborescent systems — i.e., it already makes the specific move of calling
  blockchain "arborescent" via D&G's framework, though it does not discuss
  git and is not an academic or peer-reviewed source (no citation apparatus,
  no engagement with D&G's actual text beyond the rhizome's opening lines,
  no discussion of the tracing/map principle). It is not something the
  paper is obligated to cite as scholarship, but if the paper makes the
  same blockchain-is-arborescent move, it is not the first place that
  specific comparison has appeared in public, and the author should decide
  whether to acknowledge it.
- Nothing found linking git specifically (as opposed to blockchain) to
  either side of the distinction in any D&G-informed source, academic or
  otherwise.

**Honest caveat:** this was a web-search-tool pass, not a full run through
JSTOR/Project MUSE/a university library's philosophy or STS/software-studies
databases, which are where a real peer-reviewed match — if one exists — is
most likely to actually live and least likely to surface in open web search.
This result should be read as "nothing found in open web search," not as a
definitive "nothing exists." Recommend at minimum a targeted Google Scholar
and JSTOR pass before the paper claims novelty for this comparison outright.

## Claim 4 — Is "arborescent" a fair description of git's commit history and blockchain consensus?

**Grade: ⚠️ Plausible, but needs an explicit caveat for git — the "single primary parent" framing elides git's actual DAG structure**

**Findings:**
- **Blockchain / Nakamoto consensus**: ✅ fair as described. Multiple
  sources (`decentralizedthoughts.github.io`, `eprint.iacr.org/2019/943`,
  Nervos knowledge base) confirm the longest-chain rule is exactly what the
  claim says: competing forks are resolved by adopting the chain with the
  most accumulated proof-of-work, with the explicit purpose of collapsing
  multiple candidate chains down to one canonical winner. This is a real,
  accurately-described "single trunk" consensus mechanism — the arborescent
  characterization is fair here without much qualification.
- **Git**: the claim is *not fully fair as stated* and needs a caveat the
  draft should add rather than skip. Git's actual commit history is a
  **directed acyclic graph (DAG)**, not strictly a tree: an ordinary commit
  has one parent, but a **merge commit has two or more parents** (a 3-way
  merge produces a 3-parent commit), confirmed across multiple independent
  technical sources (Medium/"Why the Git Graph is a DAG," lozeve.com "From
  graphs to Git," kindatechnical.com's DAG-model guide). A DAG with nodes
  that can have multiple parents is a strictly more general structure than
  a tree (in a tree, by definition, every node has at most one parent) — so
  "arborescent" undersells git's real graph shape. What *is* fair to say:
  (a) most individual commits (non-merges) do have exactly one parent, so
  the *common case* looks tree-like; (b) the "main"/"trunk" branch
  convention is a real, near-universal social/workflow convention layered
  on top of the DAG, not a structural constraint the DAG itself enforces;
  and (c) unlike blockchain, git has no consensus mechanism that collapses
  divergent history into one canonical line — a fork can simply stay
  forked forever, or be merged (preserving both parents), which is the
  opposite move from Nakamoto consensus's discarding of the losing chain.

**Assessment:** The paper should not claim git's structure "is" a tree
without qualification — that's a real strawman risk the adversarial-review
discipline this paper already practices (per Section 3/4's own self-
corrections) should catch. The defensible claim is narrower: git's
*ordinary, non-merge* commit relation and its social "main branch"
convention are tree-*like*/arborescent in spirit, but the underlying data
structure is a DAG that explicitly supports and preserves multiple parents,
and git — unlike blockchain — has no mechanism that forces convergence to
one canonical line. If the paper wants "arborescent" to do real
argumentative work contrasting with something else DMML does, it should
either restrict the claim to the single-parent/non-merge case explicitly,
or drop "tree" language for git in favor of "DAG with a strong single-
trunk convention," which is the accurate structural description found here.

## Overall summary

Claims 1 and 2 hold up well against real primary-adjacent sources (two
independent pages quoting the Massumi translation directly, cross-
corroborating each other): the connection/heterogeneity, multiplicity, and
asignifying-rupture principles are real and accurately summarized, and so is
the tracing-vs-mapping/genealogical-tree contrast — the one correction is
that D&G actually name six principles, not three, so the paper should either
name the full six or explicitly scope its selection as "three of six." Claim
3's search found no existing academic work applying the rhizome/arborescent
distinction to git, blockchain, or content-addressed data structures — the
comparison is not obviously already claimed in the literature I could reach,
though this was an open-web pass, not a library-database search, so treat
that as "not found," not "proven absent," and note one non-academic blog
post that already makes the narrower blockchain-is-arborescent move and
should probably be acknowledged if the paper makes the same move. Claim 4 is
fair for blockchain/Nakamoto consensus without qualification, but git's
"arborescent" label needs an explicit caveat: git's real commit graph is a
DAG that natively supports multi-parent merge commits, which is a
structurally different (more general) shape than a tree, even though the
single-parent common case and the social "main branch" convention make the
tree metaphor a reasonable simplification if flagged as such rather than
asserted flatly.
