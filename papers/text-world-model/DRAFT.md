# DMML as a World Model for Text, and How It Differs from Video World Models

*ML/cognitive-science framing, not philosophy of technology. Every citation to the ML "world models" literature below is marked with its verification status; nothing here should be taken as settled scholarship until the pending items are resolved. The drafting history behind this text lives in this repository's own commit log and `dev-journal/`, not in the paper.*

## Abstract

"World model" names two structurally different things that happen to share a name. In the machine-learning literature since Ha and Schmidhuber (2018), a world model is a learned, continuous, largely opaque latent dynamics function a policy or generator rolls forward — recent systems in this lineage (Google DeepMind's Genie line) generate real-time, promptable, action-controllable video. In DMML, a world model is an explicit, discrete, symbolic graph of triples plus declared rules for how they change. This paper states that difference precisely rather than treating either side as simply better: DMML can inspect, verify provenance for, and compose its own state in ways a learned latent model's own literature identifies as open problems, not solved ones; a learned world model can generate a photorealistic, temporally consistent scene that DMML cannot produce at all. We argue the interesting relationship is compositional, not competitive — a symbolic world model supplying structure a video world model could render — and mark that claim as speculative throughout, not a result. Closing the paper: DMML's discrete representational commitment is itself one stratum in a nested stack, not a single freestanding layer, serving as substratum for the commit graph built on top of it in the sense this paper's companion piece argues; a continuous latent representation starts a different stack, not a less-coded one — neither is closer to some raw, unrepresented world.

## 1. What "world model" means in the ML literature

The term "world model," in this exact sense, is not David Ha and Jürgen Schmidhuber's own coinage, and the paper that popularized its current currency should not be cited as its origin. Jürgen Schmidhuber's 1990 technical report ("Making the World Differentiable...," FKI-126-90, TU München; the planning portion peer-reviewed at IJCNN'90) introduced a predictor network (M) paired with a controller network (C) — the same M/C architecture Ha and Schmidhuber's 2018 paper revives and scales with modern components.<sup>[verified 2026-08-25, secondary sourcing plus Schmidhuber's own repeated, public priority claims; primary 1990 technical report itself not directly fetched]</sup> What Ha and Schmidhuber (2018) genuinely originates is the specific modern instantiation this paper actually contrasts DMML against: a VAE compresses each observation into a low-dimensional latent code, an RNN models the temporal dynamics of that latent code, and a small policy is trained on top — including, in the paper's own framing, "entirely inside of its own hallucinated dream generated by its world model."<sup>[verified 2026-08-24, arXiv:1803.10122, direct abstract check]</sup> Credit accordingly: the term and core architecture to Schmidhuber 1990, the modern latent-generative instantiation to Ha and Schmidhuber 2018.

The more recent, higher-profile instances of the same lineage generate video or persistent 3D structure directly rather than a compressed latent code a downstream policy consumes. Google DeepMind's Genie 3, announced August 2025, is "a general purpose world model that can generate an unprecedented diversity of interactive environments," navigable in real time at 24 frames per second, promptable, and action-controllable via directional navigation, holding visual consistency "for a few minutes" at 720p resolution — DeepMind's own stated framing, not outside characterization.<sup>[verified 2026-08-24, deepmind.google blog post, direct fetch]</sup> Genie 1 and 2 (2024) are real, earlier, non-real-time predecessors in the same numbered sequence.<sup>[same]</sup> As of this writing the field has forked into two distinct branches worth distinguishing rather than treating Genie 3 as the sole current anchor: video-stream world models like Genie 3, and persistent, *editable* geometric world models like World Labs' "Marble" (launched commercially Nov 2025; text/image/video/panorama input to persistent 3D environments exportable as Gaussian splats or meshes, importable into standard game engines) — a fork the trade press covering both explicitly names.<sup>[verified 2026-08-25, World Labs' own blog and TechCrunch coverage; no peer-reviewed technical paper found for Marble specifically, treat as industry-announcement-sourced, weaker than Genie 3's or Ha/Schmidhuber's citation strength]</sup> DMML's own persistent, addressable, editable symbolic state arguably makes Marble the more structurally apt point of contrast for this paper's purposes than Genie 3's video stream, even though Genie 3 remains the better-evidenced, more rigorously described system of the two.

What both share, and what makes the comparison in this paper non-trivial rather than a false-friend wordplay: both are called "world models" specifically because each is used the same functional way — as something a policy, a player, or a downstream process can query or roll forward to find out what happens next, without that next state having been hand-authored in advance for every possible action. That functional role is genuinely shared. How each represents "what is true" and "what happens next" internally is where they diverge completely, and the divergence is a real, recognized axis in AI more broadly, not one this paper invents from nothing: De Raedt, Dumančić, Manhaeve, and Marra's neurosymbolic-AI survey organizes the field along a symbolic-vs-subsymbolic representational axis as one of its core structuring dimensions, and the same divide is old enough in cognitive science that a 2003 review could already describe it as "continuing for thirty years, with little indication of a resolution."<sup>[verified 2026-08-25: De Raedt et al., arXiv:2108.11451 / *Artificial Intelligence* vol. 328 (2024); Kelley, "Symbolic and Sub-Symbolic Representations in Computational Models of Human Cognition," *Theory & Psychology* (2003)]</sup> This paper's contribution is not identifying that the divide exists, which is well established; it is making the comparison precise for this one specific pair of systems.

## 2. The actual structural difference: learned latent continuous dynamics vs. an explicit symbolic commit graph

A video world model's internal representation is a learned, high-dimensional, continuous latent state, and its transition function is a large neural network trained end-to-end on data — nothing in that pipeline is designed to be human-legible, and querying "why did the model produce this frame" is itself an open research problem (interpretability), not a built-in capability of the architecture. DMML's world state, by contrast, is an explicit set of `(subject, predicate, value)` triples, and its transitions are `commit` blocks: `consumes` names exactly what a state change depended on, `produces` names exactly what it asserts, and both are plain, inspectable data — not activations inside a trained network. `dmml/examples/pantheon.rs`, written and run for the companion ontology paper, demonstrates this concretely rather than as an abstract claim: every one of its four checks queries the *actual* materialized state after a real commit sequence and asserts on the literal result, not on a description of what should happen.

This is a genuine tradeoff, not a one-sided win, and the paper states the cost plainly rather than only the benefit. DMML cannot generate a photorealistic frame, or anything resembling one, under any circumstance — its entire representational vocabulary is symbolic triples, with no perceptual/visual component at all. A video world model, conversely, cannot (without substantial additional, separately-trained infrastructure bolted on) name *why* a given transition happened in a form a downstream reasoner could check, or guarantee that a transition respects any declared rule at all — Genie 3's own described consistency window ("a few minutes" before drift) is itself evidence that even the generative side of this tradeoff is bounded, not a claim that DMML's own tradeoff is somehow free.

## 3. Verifiability and provenance

DMML's commits chain back through explicit reference: a `consumes` entry names the specific prior fact or commit a new state depends on, and `via`/`respondsTo` name the specific commit that authorized or answered a given production. This does not, on its own, mean every DMML transition is proven correct or beyond dispute — the companion ontology paper's own Section 1 states plainly that the portable reference implementation's citation check is a no-op on a fabricated reference, not a rejection, and that stronger admission-time guarantees are a substrate-specific concern (written-world's atproto layer), not a property of DMML's grammar itself. What genuinely holds regardless: every DMML transition names, explicitly and inspectably, what it claims to depend on. A reader — human or automated — can always ask "what did this commit consume" and get a literal, structural answer, whether or not that answer turns out to be accurate.

A learned world model's rollout does not have an equivalent artifact to point to, and there is now real, directly on-topic evidence for this rather than architectural inference alone. Balogh and Jelasity (2026) test whether sequence models trained on move prediction actually respect the true rule structure of the game they were trained on, using chess as a testbed and adversarial sequence generation to probe soundness; they find none of the tested models are fully sound against adversarial sequences, and — more directly relevant here — that linear probes over the model's internal board-state representation have no causal role in next-token prediction for most of the models tested.<sup>[verified 2026-08-25, arXiv:2602.05903, accepted ICLR 2026, direct abstract/fetch]</sup> That is a structural, not incidental, finding: whatever representation of "the state of the world" such a model may be said to carry, it is not the thing the model's own output is actually conditioned on in a checkable way — the opposite of DMML's `consumes`, which names the precise dependency a transition is conditioned on as a matter of grammar, not as something a probe has to go looking for and might not find. This paper does not additionally claim that same-seed rollouts from a world model reliably diverge from one another; no citable literature for that specific claim was found, and the paper rests the provenance argument on the auditability finding above instead, which is both stronger and independently verified. What genuinely holds: DMML's history is the kind of thing that *can* be checked, queried, and disputed by a downstream party in the way `consumes` structurally guarantees; a learned world model's rollout, per the finding above, is not, even when the model appears to have learned something resembling an internal world state.

## 4. Discreteness and the open/closed predicate vocabulary

DMML's predicate vocabulary is closed by default and opened only by explicit self-declaration: a `commit` may only use `rdf:type` or a predicate `declare`d somewhere in that same commit, checked mechanically (`dmml::validate::validate_declarations`). This is a genuinely different representational commitment from a continuous latent space, with a real, checkable consequence: a new predicate, once declared, composes with every existing predicate by construction — nothing about adding `canonicalOrigin` to a world that already had `origin` (`pantheon.rs`'s own council commit) required retraining, fine-tuning, or any process that could silently degrade unrelated existing capabilities. A latent model's own generalization behavior is a different, and actively studied, kind of problem: whether a system trained on some combination of features can correctly represent and act on a *novel* combination it wasn't directly trained on is a live research question in exactly this literature, not a solved baseline. Real work exists specifically inside the continuous-latent paradigm pursuing this: Kipf, van der Pol, and Welling's contrastive structured world models (C-SWM) learn object-centric latent representations via a graph neural network rather than a single monolithic vector; Veerapaneni et al.'s entity-abstraction framework (OP3) does the same with a fully probabilistic, entity-centric latent-variable model, generalizing from training on falling blocks to novel block-stacking tasks it wasn't directly trained on; Zhao, Kong, Walters, and Wong go furthest toward naming the target directly, algebraically formalizing "compositional generalization" for object-oriented world models (HOWM).<sup>[verified 2026-08-25: C-SWM, arXiv:1911.12247, ICLR 2020; OP3, arXiv:1910.12827; HOWM, arXiv:2204.13661, ICML 2022]</sup> A fourth real paper, PoE-World (Piriyakulkij et al., arXiv:2505.10819, NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight), is worth naming for a different reason and should not be cited as a latent-space example: it represents a world model as a product of LLM-synthesized *programs*, not neural latent vectors, making it closer kin to DMML's own symbolic commitment than to the continuous-latent lineage this section is contrasting DMML against — real, independent evidence that compositional structure is worth pursuing even from researchers starting from the opposite, program-structured side of this same divide. None of this literature frames the comparison in exactly the terms this section wants — discrete/symbolic self-declared vocabulary vs. continuous latent space as *the* dividing line — and that framing should be stated plainly as this paper's own synthesis, not attributed to the cited work: the cited papers establish that compositional generalization is a real, recognized gap researchers are actively trying to close from within the latent paradigm (C-SWM, OP3, HOWM) and, separately, that at least one recent effort abandons the latent paradigm for this exact problem instead (PoE-World); the specific comparison to DMML's self-declaration mechanism is this paper's own argument built on those facts, not a restatement of them.

## 5. What DMML is worse at, honestly

DMML has no perceptual grounding of any kind. It cannot produce an image, cannot infer what a scene should look like from data, and has no mechanism for learning visual, auditory, or physical regularities from examples the way a video world model's entire training process is built to do. A DMML world is legible to a symbolic reasoner — a human reading `consumes`/`produces` blocks, or another program walking the graph — and is not merely less legible as an image than a video world model's output; it is not an image at all, and nothing about its design moves it toward becoming one. Genie 3's headline capability — generating a visually coherent, temporally consistent, real-time-navigable environment from a prompt — has no DMML analog and is not something a future version of DMML, however extended, would obviously grow into without becoming a fundamentally different kind of system. Stating this plainly matters more here than in most sections of this paper: a paper arguing for a symbolic model's real virtues reads as advocacy, not analysis, if it doesn't also state the symbolic model's real absences with the same directness.

## 6. Where the two could compose, not compete

A speculative closing point, explicitly labeled as such and not argued for at the same evidentiary standard as the sections above: the two kinds of world model may be more useful combined than as competitors for the same job. A DMML-style symbolic layer could supply the *structure* a scene needs to be coherent — what exists, what is currently true, what rules govern legal transitions — while a video world model supplies the *rendering*, turning a declared state and a declared transition into a visually and temporally coherent frame sequence, rather than the video model having to infer structure and consistency purely from its own learned priors. This would let a video world model's consistency window (Genie 3's "few minutes" before drift) be extended by an external, explicit source of truth to check against, rather than relying solely on the model's own latent memory — genuinely useful if it works, but this paper has not built it, has not verified any existing system does this, and does not claim more than that the idea is worth stating and flagging as unbuilt.

## 7. Symbolic representation is a further stratum, not the only one

Sections 2 and 4 argue DMML's discrete, closed-then-self-declared predicate vocabulary against a continuous latent representation as a structural tradeoff. This paper's companion, "DMML as a Desiring-Production Ontology," argues DMML's commit graph has a real correspondence to Deleuze and Guattari's apparatus of strata and double articulation. Framing that correspondence as "DMML is a stratum" would misstate what Deleuze and Guattari's own text says, and needs correcting rather than left implicit: strata, for them, are never freestanding or singular. "Each stratum serves as the substratum for another stratum," with no fixed order to that relation<sup>[verified 2026-08-25; Massumi translation, p. 73, p. 64]</sup> — a nested, relative stack, not a single layer sitting directly on some raw unrepresented ground. What the two papers actually connect at is that stack, not a single point on it: DMML's whole representational commitment to discrete, symbolic, subject-predicate-object triples is itself one stratum, and it serves as the *substratum* for the commit graph's own content built on top of it — Helios's, Selene's, and Eos's specific facts (`pantheon.rs`), Nyx's synthesis, and the council's declaration are each a further stratum for which the one below serves as substratum, exactly the "no fixed order, one stratum can serve directly as a substratum for another" relation the primary text describes. Choosing to represent a world as `(subject, predicate, value)` triples at all is a first-articulation-shaped move at the base of that stack, selecting a determinate, closed set of relations out of whatever "the state of a world" is prior to being forced into that shape; `dmml::validate::validate_declarations`'s closed-vocabulary check is a second, folding each selection into a stable, checkable structure the moment it's declared — and everything built on DMML afterward is a further stratum resting on that one, not a separate, unrelated fact about DMML. This is not a new claim about DMML's mechanics — Section 4 of the companion paper already establishes double articulation as a real, checkable structural analogy, with its own stated limits (it is a general pattern, found in SQL and git as readily as in DMML, not a DMML-specific discovery) — it is the same analogy pointed one level further down the stack, at DMML's representational form rather than only at facts within it.

This reframes, rather than resolves, the tradeoff Sections 2 and 4 describe. A continuous latent representation is not thereby the more "natural" or less coded of the two options, which would be an easy and wrong place to land: it is not raw, unformed experience either, only a different texture of capture — coded by gradient descent and a training objective instead of by an explicit grammar, its own first and second articulation performed by a loss function rather than a `declare` statement, and, by the same relativity, itself a base stratum for whatever a downstream policy or generator builds on top of it. Neither representation is closer to whatever "the world" is prior to being represented; both start a stack of strata, differing in what does the capturing at the base and how visible the capturing stays as further strata accumulate on top of it — which is precisely where the honest version of DMML's real advantage lives, not in DMML being somehow unstratified, and not in DMML being reducible to one single stratum either.

Two philosophers earn a place here, each for a specific, checkable reason rather than as ornament, and both with real limits stated rather than smoothed over. Nietzsche's *On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense* describes concept-formation directly in these terms: "Every concept originates through our equating what is unequal"<sup>[verified 2026-08-25; Breazeale translation, "Philosophy and Truth"]</sup> — a general category manufactured by discarding the individual, non-repeating differences between particular cases — and argues the constructed origin of a concept is then *forgotten*, so that "truths are illusions about which one has forgotten that this is what they are; metaphors which are worn out."<sup>[verified 2026-08-25; Kaufmann translation, *The Portable Nietzsche*]</sup> This is close enough to D&G's own capture-of-flows vocabulary to be a real, load-bearing resonance, though it should be stated plainly as this paper's own thematic connection rather than an inherited scholarly lineage: Deleuze and Guattari's own cited source for double articulation is the linguist Louis Hjelmslev, not Nietzsche, and no secondary literature connecting Nietzsche's concept-forgetting argument to D&G's strata specifically was found in a real search pass.<sup>[verified 2026-08-25]</sup> What the resonance does buy the argument is a concrete, checkable point about DMML specifically: DMML's own self-declaration mechanism is close to the opposite of what Nietzsche diagnoses. Every predicate's coding act is a permanent, dated, attributable `declare` statement remaining in the log — the moment a concept was minted is never forgotten the way Nietzsche's ordinary concept-use forgets its own metaphorical origin, because DMML has no mechanism for a declaration to silently naturalize into an unmarked, un-owned fact of the grammar. Whether that structural difference matters for anything beyond DMML's own auditability (Section 3) is a further question this paper does not pursue past naming it.

Wittgenstein appears on both sides of this argument's own history and should not be flattened into supporting only one. The early Wittgenstein of the *Tractatus* holds that a proposition can only picture a fact by sharing its logical form — "What every picture... must have in common with reality in order to be able to represent it at all... is the logical form, that is, the form of reality"<sup>[verified 2026-08-25; Ogden translation, 2.18]</sup> — and that "the limits of my language mean the limits of my world" (5.6). This supports the coding-as-precondition half of the claim precisely: a symbolic system's form is not a neutral window onto a pre-given world, it is what makes saying anything about that world possible or impossible in the first place, exactly the role a stratum plays for whatever it captures. But the later Wittgenstein of the *Philosophical Investigations* explicitly repudiates this — replacing one determinate logical form with an irreducible multiplicity of local "language-games," each embedded in its own "form of life," no single one of them underwriting the rest. That later position is closer to D&G's own anti-arborescent multiplicity than to a single stratifying operation, and using it to support a stratification reading at all would misrepresent it. Worth stating plainly rather than glossing: Deleuze's own recorded view of Wittgenstein was hostile, treating language-game analysis as a therapeutic containment of thought opposed to his own commitment to concept-creation.<sup>[verified 2026-08-25]</sup> Invoking Wittgenstein here is this paper's own synthesis, not a connection Deleuze and Guattari's own reception of him would have licensed — the early Tractatus genuinely supports one half of this section's claim, and the later Wittgenstein's disagreement with his own earlier self is itself instructive: even the philosopher who wrote the sharpest statement of language-as-coding came to think one fixed code was the wrong picture, in favor of exactly the kind of multiplicity DMML's own default (Section 4 of the companion paper: no forced convergence, permanent coexistence of rival claims) already builds in rather than argues for after the fact.
