# DMML as a World Model for Text, and How It Differs from Video World Models

*ML/cognitive-science framing, not philosophy of technology. Every citation to the ML "world models" literature below is marked with its verification status; nothing here should be taken as settled scholarship until the pending items are resolved. The drafting history behind this text lives in this repository's own commit log and `dev-journal/`, not in the paper.*

## Abstract

"World model" names two structurally different things that happen to share a name. In the machine-learning literature since Ha and Schmidhuber (2018), a world model is a learned, continuous, largely opaque latent dynamics function a policy or generator rolls forward — recent systems in this lineage (Google DeepMind's Genie line) generate real-time, promptable, action-controllable video. In DMML, a world model is an explicit, discrete, symbolic graph of triples plus declared rules for how they change. This paper states that difference precisely rather than treating either side as simply better: DMML can inspect, verify provenance for, and compose its own state in ways a learned latent model's own literature identifies as open problems, not solved ones; a learned world model can generate a photorealistic, temporally consistent scene that DMML cannot produce at all. We argue the interesting relationship is compositional, not competitive — a symbolic world model supplying structure a video world model could render — and mark that claim as speculative throughout, not a result.

## 1. What "world model" means in the ML literature

David Ha and Jürgen Schmidhuber's "World Models" (2018) is the paper most commonly credited with the term's current currency in reinforcement learning: a VAE compresses each observation into a low-dimensional latent code, an RNN models the temporal dynamics of that latent code, and a small policy is trained on top — including, in the paper's own framing, "entirely inside of its own hallucinated dream generated by its world model."<sup>[verified 2026-08-24, arXiv:1803.10122, direct abstract check]</sup> Whether this is genuinely the origin of the term, or a later, especially influential instance of an older model-based-control lineage, is a real question this paper takes seriously rather than assumes<sup>[pending: a research pass is checking Schmidhuber's own earlier 1990s world-model/controller work and the broader model-based-RL lineage (Sutton's Dyna architecture and related) for a more accurate origin story before this section is finalized]</sup>.

The more recent, higher-profile instances of the same lineage generate video directly rather than a compressed latent code a downstream policy consumes. Google DeepMind's Genie 3, announced August 2025, is "a general purpose world model that can generate an unprecedented diversity of interactive environments," navigable in real time at 24 frames per second, promptable, and action-controllable via directional navigation, holding visual consistency "for a few minutes" at 720p resolution — DeepMind's own stated framing, not outside characterization.<sup>[verified 2026-08-24, deepmind.google blog post, direct fetch]</sup> Genie 1 and 2 (2024) are real, earlier, non-real-time predecessors in the same numbered sequence.<sup>[same]</sup> Whether anything more recent than Genie 3 has since become the field's better current anchor is checked below rather than assumed from a training cutoff<sup>[pending research pass]</sup>.

What both share, and what makes the comparison in this paper non-trivial rather than a false-friend wordplay: both are called "world models" specifically because each is used the same functional way — as something a policy, a player, or a downstream process can query or roll forward to find out what happens next, without that next state having been hand-authored in advance for every possible action. That functional role is genuinely shared. How each represents "what is true" and "what happens next" internally is where they diverge completely.

## 2. The actual structural difference: learned latent continuous dynamics vs. an explicit symbolic commit graph

A video world model's internal representation is a learned, high-dimensional, continuous latent state, and its transition function is a large neural network trained end-to-end on data — nothing in that pipeline is designed to be human-legible, and querying "why did the model produce this frame" is itself an open research problem (interpretability), not a built-in capability of the architecture. DMML's world state, by contrast, is an explicit set of `(subject, predicate, value)` triples, and its transitions are `commit` blocks: `consumes` names exactly what a state change depended on, `produces` names exactly what it asserts, and both are plain, inspectable data — not activations inside a trained network. `dmml/examples/pantheon.rs`, written and run for the companion ontology paper, demonstrates this concretely rather than as an abstract claim: every one of its four checks queries the *actual* materialized state after a real commit sequence and asserts on the literal result, not on a description of what should happen.

This is a genuine tradeoff, not a one-sided win, and the paper states the cost plainly rather than only the benefit. DMML cannot generate a photorealistic frame, or anything resembling one, under any circumstance — its entire representational vocabulary is symbolic triples, with no perceptual/visual component at all. A video world model, conversely, cannot (without substantial additional, separately-trained infrastructure bolted on) name *why* a given transition happened in a form a downstream reasoner could check, or guarantee that a transition respects any declared rule at all — Genie 3's own described consistency window ("a few minutes" before drift) is itself evidence that even the generative side of this tradeoff is bounded, not a claim that DMML's own tradeoff is somehow free.

## 3. Verifiability and provenance

DMML's commits chain back through explicit reference: a `consumes` entry names the specific prior fact or commit a new state depends on, and `via`/`respondsTo` name the specific commit that authorized or answered a given production. This does not, on its own, mean every DMML transition is proven correct or beyond dispute — the companion ontology paper's own Section 1 states plainly that the portable reference implementation's citation check is a no-op on a fabricated reference, not a rejection, and that stronger admission-time guarantees are a substrate-specific concern (written-world's atproto layer), not a property of DMML's grammar itself. What genuinely holds regardless: every DMML transition names, explicitly and inspectably, what it claims to depend on. A reader — human or automated — can always ask "what did this commit consume" and get a literal, structural answer, whether or not that answer turns out to be accurate.

A learned world model's rollout does not have an equivalent artifact to point to. Two rollouts from nominally "the same" prompt or seed can diverge — a consequence of stochastic sampling, floating-point non-determinism across hardware, or simply the model's own learned uncertainty — and nothing in the architecture produces a structured record of *why* one continuation was generated rather than another.<sup>[pending: a research pass is checking for real, citable literature specifically on reproducibility/determinism in world-model or video-generation rollouts, rather than resting this claim on architectural inference alone]</sup> This is the sharper, more defensible version of the provenance claim: not that DMML's history is trustworthy in some absolute sense, but that DMML's history is the kind of thing that *can* be checked, queried, and disputed by a downstream party, and a video world model's rollout, as currently architected, is not.

## 4. Discreteness and the open/closed predicate vocabulary

DMML's predicate vocabulary is closed by default and opened only by explicit self-declaration: a `commit` may only use `rdf:type` or a predicate `declare`d somewhere in that same commit, checked mechanically (`dmml::validate::validate_declarations`). This is a genuinely different representational commitment from a continuous latent space, with a real, checkable consequence: a new predicate, once declared, composes with every existing predicate by construction — nothing about adding `canonicalOrigin` to a world that already had `origin` (`pantheon.rs`'s own council commit) required retraining, fine-tuning, or any process that could silently degrade unrelated existing capabilities. A latent model's own generalization behavior is a different, and actively studied, kind of problem: whether a system trained on some combination of features can correctly represent and act on a *novel* combination it wasn't directly trained on is a live research question in exactly this literature, not a solved baseline. Real work exists in this specific direction — "PoE-World: Compositional World Modeling with Products of Programmatic Experts" and "Toward Compositional Generalization in Object-Oriented World Modeling" are both real, findable papers pursuing compositional structure specifically because a monolithic latent model doesn't get it for free.<sup>[verified 2026-08-24: PoE-World, arXiv:2505.10819; "Toward Compositional Generalization in Object-Oriented World Modeling" (Zhao, Kong et al.); related: "Inter-environmental world modeling for continuous and compositional dynamics," arXiv:2503.09911]</sup> None of this literature frames the comparison in exactly the terms this section wants — discrete/symbolic self-declared vocabulary vs. continuous latent space as *the* dividing line — and that framing should be stated plainly as this paper's own synthesis, not attributed to the cited work: the cited papers establish that compositional generalization is a real, recognized gap researchers are actively trying to close in latent models, which is the load-bearing fact this section actually needs; the specific comparison to DMML's self-declaration mechanism is this paper's own argument built on that fact, not a restatement of it.

## 5. What DMML is worse at, honestly

DMML has no perceptual grounding of any kind. It cannot produce an image, cannot infer what a scene should look like from data, and has no mechanism for learning visual, auditory, or physical regularities from examples the way a video world model's entire training process is built to do. A DMML world is legible to a symbolic reasoner — a human reading `consumes`/`produces` blocks, or another program walking the graph — and is not merely less legible as an image than a video world model's output; it is not an image at all, and nothing about its design moves it toward becoming one. Genie 3's headline capability — generating a visually coherent, temporally consistent, real-time-navigable environment from a prompt — has no DMML analog and is not something a future version of DMML, however extended, would obviously grow into without becoming a fundamentally different kind of system. Stating this plainly matters more here than in most sections of this paper: a paper arguing for a symbolic model's real virtues reads as advocacy, not analysis, if it doesn't also state the symbolic model's real absences with the same directness.

## 6. Where the two could compose, not compete

A speculative closing point, explicitly labeled as such and not argued for at the same evidentiary standard as the sections above: the two kinds of world model may be more useful combined than as competitors for the same job. A DMML-style symbolic layer could supply the *structure* a scene needs to be coherent — what exists, what is currently true, what rules govern legal transitions — while a video world model supplies the *rendering*, turning a declared state and a declared transition into a visually and temporally coherent frame sequence, rather than the video model having to infer structure and consistency purely from its own learned priors. This would let a video world model's consistency window (Genie 3's "few minutes" before drift) be extended by an external, explicit source of truth to check against, rather than relying solely on the model's own latent memory — genuinely useful if it works, but this paper has not built it, has not verified any existing system does this, and does not claim more than that the idea is worth stating and flagging as unbuilt.
